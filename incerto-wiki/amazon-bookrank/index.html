Amazon Book Rank Tail Diagnostics

Setup¶

This empirical example uses Zhu’s CC0 Kaggle snapshot of Amazon book sales and weekly average sales ranks from 2001 Zhu, 2021. The cleaned snapshot is committed at data/snapshots/amazon_bookrank_2001.csv because it is small and redistributable.

The example asks two related questions:

  • Does weekly sales volume have a heavy right tail?

  • Does the sales-rank relation look approximately log-linear, as one would expect from a rough power-law ranking rule?

The rank variable is inverse popularity: rank 1 is better than rank 1000.

Why book ranks suggest a tail question¶

Book sales are a natural “winner-take-more” setting. Most titles sell little in a given week, while a few sell much more. A rank is not the same object as a sales count, but if sales approximately follow a power law, a log-log plot of sales against rank can look close to linear over part of the range.

The important word is “approximately.” Amazon’s ranking algorithm is not a statistical law of nature, and this old dataset is small. The example is a real-data bridge from the Pareto pages to rank-size empirical work.

Evidence and model comparison¶

There is no proof that the data are Pareto. The computation is a reproducible diagnostic:

  1. Load the CC0 snapshot.

  2. Plot weekly sales against weekly average rank on log-log axes.

  3. Plot empirical sales survival on log-log axes.

  4. Apply Hill Estimator sensitivity checks to positive weekly sales above a small reporting cutoff.

Any resulting exponent is sample-specific and should be read as an empirical description, not a structural parameter of Amazon.

Source
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import linregress

from incerto.datasets import load_amazon_bookrank
from incerto.estimators import hill_alpha_estimator, hill_stability
from incerto.figures import COLORS, FIGURE_SIZES, set_theme, style_axes

set_theme()

data = load_amazon_bookrank()
sales = data.weekly_sales
ranks = data.weekly_average_rank

mask = (sales > 0) & (ranks > 0)
log_rank = np.log(ranks[mask])
log_sales = np.log(sales[mask])
slope, intercept, r_value, _, _ = linregress(log_rank, log_sales)

positive_sales = np.sort(sales[sales > 0])
sales_survival = 1 - np.arange(1, positive_sales.size + 1) / (positive_sales.size + 1)

tail_sample = sales[sales > 10]
k_max = max(6, min(140, tail_sample.size - 1))
ks = np.unique(np.geomspace(5, k_max, 60).astype(int))
stability = hill_stability(tail_sample, ks)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=FIGURE_SIZES["three_panel"])

axes[0].scatter(ranks, sales, s=14, alpha=0.65, color=COLORS["accent"])
rank_grid = np.geomspace(ranks.min(), ranks.max(), 150)
axes[0].plot(
    rank_grid,
    np.exp(intercept) * rank_grid**slope,
    color=COLORS["umber"],
    ls="--",
    label=f"log-log slope {slope:.2f}",
)
axes[0].set_xscale("log")
axes[0].set_yscale("log")
axes[0].set_xlabel("weekly average rank")
axes[0].set_ylabel("weekly sales")
axes[0].set_title("Sales-rank relation")
axes[0].legend()

axes[1].loglog(positive_sales, sales_survival, color=COLORS["teal"])
axes[1].set_xlabel("weekly sales")
axes[1].set_ylabel("empirical survival")
axes[1].set_title("Sales survival")

axes[2].semilogx(stability["k"], stability["alpha"], color=COLORS["green"])
axes[2].set_xlabel("upper order statistics k")
axes[2].set_ylabel(r"Hill $\widehat\alpha$")
axes[2].set_title("Tail sensitivity")

style_axes(axes, grid_axis="both")
plt.show()

print(f"Rows: {sales.size}; titles dated from {data.date.min()} to {data.date.max()}")
print(f"Log-log rank/sales R^2: {r_value**2:.3f}")
for k in [10, 20, 40, 80, 120]:
    if k < tail_sample.size:
        print(f"Hill alpha on sales > 10 for k={k:3d}: {hill_alpha_estimator(tail_sample, k):.2f}")
Rows: 861; titles dated from 2001-04-21 to 2001-05-05
Log-log rank/sales R^2: 0.801
Hill alpha on sales > 10 for k= 10: 3.67
Hill alpha on sales > 10 for k= 20: 2.51
Hill alpha on sales > 10 for k= 40: 2.27
Hill alpha on sales > 10 for k= 80: 2.12
Hill alpha on sales > 10 for k=120: 2.07

What to notice. The log-log fit captures a broad rank-size direction, but the survival and Hill panels make the tail sensitivity visible. The calculation is a diagnostic bridge, not proof of a generative Pareto law.

Caveats¶

  • The snapshot is old and small. It should not be used to infer current Amazon ranking behavior.

  • The sample appears to be a narrow book universe rather than all books on Amazon.

  • A rank is generated by a platform algorithm and can be affected by time windows, category rules, stock status, and measurement conventions.

  • Weekly sales are discrete and bounded in this sample. That makes the far right tail short.

  • A log-log line over ranks is descriptive. It does not prove a generative Pareto mechanism.
