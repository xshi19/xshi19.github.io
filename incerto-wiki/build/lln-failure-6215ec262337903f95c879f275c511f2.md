# LLN Failure Under Infinite Mean

## Statement

Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be iid nonnegative random variables and let

$$
S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i.
$$

If $\mathbb E[X_1]=\infty$, then

$$
\frac{S_n}{n}\to\infty
\qquad\text{almost surely.}
$$

Thus the ordinary strong law of large numbers does not converge to a finite
population mean; there is no finite mean to converge to. For a Pareto Type I
tail, this applies when $\alpha\le1$.

When $\mathbb E[X_1]<\infty$, the strong law still gives
$S_n/n\to\mathbb E[X_1]$ almost surely. The fat-tail problem then moves from
existence to speed: for $1<\alpha\le2$, the mean exists but the variance does
not, and finite samples can remain dominated by rare jumps.

## Intuition

The thin-tail habit is to imagine that more observations steadily average away
surprises. Infinite-mean samples do the opposite: as $n$ grows, the sample has
more chances to discover a new scale of observation, and those discoveries lift
the average instead of washing out.

For $\alpha$ just above 1, the formal mean exists, but the path can still look
unstable for a long time. This is the pre-asymptotic zone emphasized throughout
SCoFT: asymptotic truth and usable finite-sample behavior are not the same
claim.

## Proof

For $m>0$, define the bounded truncation

$$
X_i^{(m)}=\min(X_i,m),
\qquad
S_n^{(m)}=\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^{(m)}.
$$

Each $X_i^{(m)}$ has finite mean, so the ordinary strong law gives

$$
\frac{S_n^{(m)}}{n}\to \mathbb E[X_1^{(m)}]
\qquad\text{almost surely.}
$$

Because $0\le X_i^{(m)}\le X_i$, we have $S_n^{(m)}\le S_n$. Therefore

$$
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{S_n}{n}
\ge
\mathbb E[X_1^{(m)}]
\qquad\text{almost surely}
$$

for every fixed $m$. Monotone convergence gives
$\mathbb E[X_1^{(m)}]\uparrow\mathbb E[X_1]=\infty$. Letting $m$ increase
forces the liminf of $S_n/n$ to be arbitrarily large, hence
$S_n/n\to\infty$ almost surely.

For a Pareto Type I variable, [Pareto Moment Existence](pareto-moment-existence.md)
shows that $\mathbb E[X]$ is infinite exactly when $\alpha\le1$.

## Python

The reusable helper `running_mean` keeps the example explicit while leaving
sampling choices visible.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import pareto
from incerto.estimators import max_to_sum_ratio, running_mean

rng = np.random.default_rng(20260523)

for alpha in [0.9, 1.1, 2.5]:
    sample = pareto.rvs(b=alpha, size=20_000, random_state=rng)
    path = running_mean(sample)
    print(alpha, path[-1], max_to_sum_ratio(sample))
```

The $\alpha=0.9$ path has no finite target. The $\alpha=1.1$ path has a target,
but a single maximum can still be a large share of the cumulative sum. The
linked [pre-asymptotic LLN example](../examples/lln-preasymptotic.md) makes
that path behavior interactive.

## Caveats

- The divergence statement above uses nonnegative variables. Signed variables
  with undefined positive and negative parts need a separate statement.
- Infinite variance is not the same as LLN failure. For $1<\alpha\le2$, the
  mean exists and the strong law holds, but convergence is slow and jumpy.
- A finite simulation cannot prove asymptotic divergence. It is an illustration
  of path behavior under a specified model.
- Dependence, truncation, and changing exposure can alter the conclusion; state
  iid and tail assumptions before applying the result.
