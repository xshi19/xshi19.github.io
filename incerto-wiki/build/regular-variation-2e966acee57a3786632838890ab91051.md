# Regular Variation

## Statement

A measurable positive function $L$ is slowly varying at infinity if, for every
fixed $t>0$,

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{L(tx)}{L(x)}=1.
$$

A survival function $\bar F$ is regularly varying with index $-\alpha$,
written $\bar F\in RV_{-\alpha}$, if

$$
\bar F(x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x)
$$

for some slowly varying $L$ and some $\alpha>0$.  Equivalently,

$$
\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\bar F(tx)}{\bar F(x)}=t^{-\alpha},
\qquad t>0.
$$

## Intuition

Regular variation is the mathematical version of Pareto-style scalability.  At
large thresholds, multiplying the threshold by $t$ has an asymptotically stable
effect on exceedance probabilities.  The slowly varying factor $L$ allows
departures from an exact Pareto law while preserving the same tail exponent.

In Chapter 3 of SCoFT, this is the formal home for the power-law intuition:
large observations are not merely "more frequent"; they remain consequential at
larger and larger scales.

## Proof

If $\bar F(x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x)$ with $L$ slowly varying, then for fixed $t>0$,

$$
\frac{\bar F(tx)}{\bar F(x)}
=\frac{(tx)^{-\alpha}L(tx)}{x^{-\alpha}L(x)}
=t^{-\alpha}\frac{L(tx)}{L(x)}
\to t^{-\alpha}.
$$

Conversely, the representation theorem for regular variation shows that a
positive measurable function satisfying the ratio limit can be represented as
$x^{-\alpha}L(x)$ for a slowly varying $L$.  This direction is a standard
theorem; the wiki cites it rather than re-proving the full representation
argument here.

For the Pareto distribution with lower cutoff $x_m$,

$$
\bar F(x)=x_m^\alpha x^{-\alpha},
$$

so $L(x)=x_m^\alpha$ is constant and therefore slowly varying.

## Python

The ratio diagnostic below checks the defining limit on a grid.  Exact Pareto
tails are flat; empirical tails should be treated as approximate and threshold
dependent.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import pareto

alpha = 1.5
t = 2.0
x = np.logspace(0, 6, 7)

ratio = pareto.sf(t * x, b=alpha) / pareto.sf(x, b=alpha)
target = t ** (-alpha)

print(np.column_stack([x, ratio]))
print(target)
```

## Caveats

- Regular variation is an asymptotic property.  It does not say that every
  moderate observation follows a power law.
- Estimating $\alpha$ from finite samples is threshold-sensitive; the Hill
  estimator and log-log plots are diagnostics, not magic certificates.
- A regularly varying right tail with $\alpha>0$ is subexponential under
  standard conditions, but the one-big-jump principle should live on its own
  concept page before being used as a theorem in guides.
- Moment implications require assumptions on the full tail and should point to
  [Karamata's theorem](karamata.md) or to a Pareto-specific proof.
