Karamata’s Theorem

Statement¶

Let LLL be eventually positive, locally bounded, and slowly varying at infinity. If ρ>−1\rho>-1ρ>−1, then

∫axtρL(t) dt∼xρ+1L(x)ρ+1,x→∞.\int_a^x t^\rho L(t)\,dt \sim \frac{x^{\rho+1}L(x)}{\rho+1}, \qquad x\to\infty.∫ax​tρL(t)dt∼ρ+1xρ+1L(x)​,x→∞.

∫x∞tρL(t) dt∼xρ+1L(x)−ρ−1,x→∞.\int_x^\infty t^\rho L(t)\,dt \sim \frac{x^{\rho+1}L(x)}{-\rho-1}, \qquad x\to\infty.∫x∞​tρL(t)dt∼−ρ−1xρ+1L(x)​,x→∞.

Thus a Pareto-type tail has finite pppth moment for p<αp<\alphap<α and infinite pppth moment for p>αp>\alphap>α. The boundary case p=αp=\alphap=α is not decided by this theorem alone; it depends on the slowly varying factor LLL.

Intuition¶

Karamata’s theorem says that, for regularly varying functions, integrals are asymptotically governed by the endpoint where the mass accumulates. When ρ>−1\rho>-1ρ>−1, the integral up to xxx is controlled by the upper endpoint. When ρ<−1\rho<-1ρ<−1, the remaining tail integral beyond xxx is controlled by the lower endpoint.

This is the bridge between tail shape and moment existence. Once the survival tail behaves like x−αL(x)x^{-\alpha}L(x)x−αL(x), multiplying by xpx^pxp tests whether the pppth moment is still dominated by ordinary observations or by the far tail.

Proof¶

The integral asymptotics are the classical form of Karamata’s theorem for regularly varying functions. The full proof uses uniform convergence of slowly varying functions on compact multiplier intervals and a split of the integral into a near-endpoint part and a negligible remainder; the wiki cites the standard theorem rather than reproducing that argument here.

The moment consequences follow from integration by parts. For 0<p<α0<p<\alpha0<p<α,

(5)

Python¶

For an exact Pareto tail, LLL is constant, so the Karamata ratios are visible without simulation.

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad

from incerto.distributions import pareto_type1

alpha = 1.5
cutoffs = np.logspace(1, 5, 5)

# Finite tail moments when p < alpha.
p = 1.0
tail_ratios = []
for x in cutoffs:
    scaled_integral = quad(lambda u: u ** (p - alpha - 1), 1.0, np.inf)[0]
    tail_moment = x**p * pareto_type1.sf(x, alpha)
    tail_moment += p * x ** (p - alpha) * scaled_integral
    asymptotic = alpha / (alpha - p) * x**p * pareto_type1.sf(x, alpha)
    tail_ratios.append(tail_moment / asymptotic)

print(f"Tail-moment ratio for p={p} < alpha: {np.round(tail_ratios, 6)}")

# Divergent lower truncations when p > alpha.
p = 2.0
truncated_ratios = []
for x in cutoffs:
    truncated = quad(lambda t: t**p * pareto_type1.pdf(t, alpha), 1.0, x)[0]
    asymptotic = alpha / (p - alpha) * x**p * pareto_type1.sf(x, alpha)
    truncated_ratios.append(truncated / asymptotic)

print(f"Lower-truncation ratio for p={p} > alpha: {np.round(truncated_ratios, 6)}")

The first ratio is exactly 1 for an exact Pareto tail; the lower-truncation ratio approaches 1 as the cutoff grows.

Caveats¶

  • The assumptions on LLL matter. Slow variation is an asymptotic regularity condition, not a finite-sample diagnostic.

  • Karamata does not settle the boundary moment p=αp=\alphap=α. Exact Pareto tails diverge logarithmically there, while other slowly varying factors can change the boundary behavior.

  • Moment existence is a tail statement. Estimating a moment from data also depends on sample size, dependence, threshold choice, and whether the fitted tail model is credible.

  • This page treats nonnegative right tails. Two-sided models need the same reasoning applied to ∣X∣|X|∣X∣ or to each side separately.
