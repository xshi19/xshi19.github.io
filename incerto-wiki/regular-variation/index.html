Regular Variation

Statement¶

A measurable positive function LLL is slowly varying at infinity if, for every fixed t>0t>0t>0,

lim⁡x→∞L(tx)L(x)=1.\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{L(tx)}{L(x)}=1.x→∞lim​L(x)L(tx)​=1.
(1)

A survival function Fˉ\bar FFˉ is regularly varying with index −α-\alpha−α, written Fˉ∈RV−α\bar F\in RV_{-\alpha}Fˉ∈RV−α​, if

Fˉ(x)=x−αL(x)\bar F(x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x)Fˉ(x)=x−αL(x)
(2)

for some slowly varying LLL and some α>0\alpha>0α>0. Equivalently,

lim⁡x→∞Fˉ(tx)Fˉ(x)=t−α,t>0.\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\bar F(tx)}{\bar F(x)}=t^{-\alpha}, \qquad t>0.x→∞lim​Fˉ(x)Fˉ(tx)​=t−α,t>0.
(3)

Intuition¶

Regular variation is the mathematical version of Pareto-style scalability. At large thresholds, multiplying the threshold by ttt has an asymptotically stable effect on exceedance probabilities. The slowly varying factor LLL allows departures from an exact Pareto law while preserving the same tail exponent.

In Chapter 3 of SCoFT, this is the formal home for the power-law intuition: large observations are not merely “more frequent”; they remain consequential at larger and larger scales.

Proof¶

If Fˉ(x)=x−αL(x)\bar F(x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x)Fˉ(x)=x−αL(x) with LLL slowly varying, then for fixed t>0t>0t>0,

Fˉ(tx)Fˉ(x)=(tx)−αL(tx)x−αL(x)=t−αL(tx)L(x)→t−α.\frac{\bar F(tx)}{\bar F(x)} =\frac{(tx)^{-\alpha}L(tx)}{x^{-\alpha}L(x)} =t^{-\alpha}\frac{L(tx)}{L(x)} \to t^{-\alpha}.Fˉ(x)Fˉ(tx)​=x−αL(x)(tx)−αL(tx)​=t−αL(x)L(tx)​→t−α.
(4)

Conversely, the representation theorem for regular variation shows that a positive measurable function satisfying the ratio limit can be represented as x−αL(x)x^{-\alpha}L(x)x−αL(x) for a slowly varying LLL. This direction is a standard theorem; the wiki cites it rather than re-proving the full representation argument here.

For the Pareto distribution with lower cutoff xmx_mxm​,

Fˉ(x)=xmαx−α,\bar F(x)=x_m^\alpha x^{-\alpha},Fˉ(x)=xmα​x−α,
(5)

so L(x)=xmαL(x)=x_m^\alphaL(x)=xmα​ is constant and therefore slowly varying.

Python¶

The ratio diagnostic below checks the defining limit on a grid. Exact Pareto tails are flat; empirical tails should be treated as approximate and threshold dependent.

import numpy as np

from incerto.distributions import pareto_type1

alpha = 1.5
t = 2.0
x = np.logspace(0, 6, 7)

ratio = pareto_type1.sf(t * x, alpha) / pareto_type1.sf(x, alpha)
target = t ** (-alpha)

print("Survival-ratio diagnostic:")
for threshold, observed in zip(x, ratio):
    print(f"  x={threshold:9.0f}  ratio={observed:.6f}")
print(f"Target ratio t^(-alpha): {target:.6f}")

Caveats¶

  • Regular variation is an asymptotic property. It does not say that every moderate observation follows a power law.

  • Estimating α\alphaα from finite samples is threshold-sensitive; the Hill estimator and log-log plots are diagnostics, not magic certificates.

  • A regularly varying right tail with α>0\alpha>0α>0 is subexponential under standard conditions, but the one-big-jump principle should live on its own concept page before being used as a theorem in guides.

  • Moment implications require assumptions on the full tail and should point to Karamata’s theorem or to a Pareto-specific proof.
