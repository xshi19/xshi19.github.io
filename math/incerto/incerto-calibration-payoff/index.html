Probability calibration versus payoff accuracy

A probability forecast describes an event. A loss forecast describes its consequences. Even a perfectly calibrated event probability leaves the conditional severity unspecified. The asymmetric-payoff note already separates gain frequency from expected gain; here we derive the sampling laws of event scores and compare them with unbounded exposures.

The source context is SCoFT v4, chapter 11, §§11.1–11.5 and §11.7, printed pp. 220–240 Taleb, 2025. The calculations below are original model arguments. They do not establish a result about participants in psychology experiments, forecasters, or financial markets.

Specify the event and its payoff¶

Let X≥0X\ge0X≥0 be a loss amount, u>0u>0u>0 a fixed threshold, and D=1{X>u}D=\mathbf1_{\{X>u\}}D=1{X>u}​. We use the strict event and the shared survival notation pu=Fˉ(u)p_u=\bar F(u)pu​=Fˉ(u). A binary contract pays one unit when D=1D=1D=1; a vanilla excess contract pays (X−u)+=max⁡(X−u,0)(X-u)_+=\max(X-u,0)(X−u)+​=max(X−u,0). Its expected payoff is not an option price unless a pricing measure, discounting convention, and contract costs have also been specified.

Assume pu>0p_u>0pu​>0. Write T(u)T(u)T(u) for the unconditional tail-level mean and C(u)C(u)C(u) for the unconditional excess mean. We prove their decomposition:

T(u)=E[XD],C(u)=E[(X−u)+],T(u)=upu+C(u),C(u)=pue(u).\begin{gathered} T(u)=\mathbb E[X D],\\ C(u)=\mathbb E[(X-u)_+],\\ T(u)=u p_u+C(u),\\ C(u)=p_u e(u). \end{gathered}T(u)=E[XD],C(u)=E[(X−u)+​],T(u)=upu​+C(u),C(u)=pu​e(u).​
(1)

Indeed, XD=uD+(X−u)+XD=uD+(X-u)_+XD=uD+(X−u)+​ pointwise. Taking nonnegative expectations proves the third line even when a mean is infinite. Conditioning the excess on D=1D=1D=1 proves the fourth, with e(u)e(u)e(u) the mean excess. Thus replacing T(u)T(u)T(u) by upuup_uupu​ omits the entire excess contribution.

A threshold, horizon, units, and observation rule can define a disaster indicator under any tail law. Fat tails do not make measurable events undefined. Words such as “collapse” alone do not specify those choices, and a specified indicator does not encode all losses above its threshold. A fixed cash amount plus one binary payoff is constant on X>uX>uX>u; it cannot equal (X−u)+(X-u)_+(X−u)+​ on an unbounded support. This proves a static nonreplication statement for those two instruments. It is not a theorem about every dynamic trading strategy. Pointwise, a continuum of thresholds can recover the excess:

(x−u)+=∫u∞1{x>v} dv.(x-u)_+=\int_u^\infty\mathbf1_{\{x>v\}}\,dv.(x−u)+​=∫u∞​1{x>v}​dv.
(2)

Equal event probabilities leave severity open¶

For an exact Pareto law with u≥xm>0u\ge x_m>0u≥xm​>0 and α>1\alpha>1α>1, the Pareto moment calculation gives

pu=(xm/u)α,C(u)=upuα−1,T(u)upu=αα−1.\begin{gathered} p_u=(x_m/u)^\alpha,\\ C(u)=\frac{u p_u}{\alpha-1},\\ \frac{T(u)}{u p_u}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}. \end{gathered}pu​=(xm​/u)α,C(u)=α−1upu​​,upu​T(u)​=α−1α​.​
(3)

The excess formula also follows directly by integrating C(u)=∫u∞Fˉ(v) dvC(u)=\int_u^\infty\bar F(v)\,dvC(u)=∫u∞​Fˉ(v)dv, justified by the preceding indicator identity and Tonelli’s theorem. For α≤1\alpha\le1α≤1, both payoff means are infinite. The binary payoff remains bounded in every case.

For an exponential loss of mean b>0b>0b>0, the same integration yields C(u)=bpuC(u)=b p_uC(u)=bpu​ and T(u)/(upu)=1+b/uT(u)/(up_u)=1+b/uT(u)/(upu​)=1+b/u. These exact models explain why substituting the threshold for conditional severity can have very different relative errors. The limiting ratio alone is not a definition of all thin or heavy tails. The Weibull example with survival exp⁡(−x)\exp(-\sqrt x)exp(−x​) is subexponential, but its mean excess 2u+22\sqrt u+22u​+2 gives T(u)/(upu)→1T(u)/(up_u)\to1T(u)/(upu​)→1. See also the mean-excess corollary for the hypotheses needed to pass from tail-law limits to means.

For any fixed 0<p<10<p<10<p<1 and u>0u>0u>0, choose xm=up1/αx_m=u p^{1/\alpha}xm​=up1/α. All these Pareto laws have the same event probability ppp, while C(u)=up/(α−1)C(u)=up/(\alpha-1)C(u)=up/(α−1) is unbounded as α↓1\alpha\downarrow1α↓1. No statistic of this single indicator alone identifies that payoff mean. Defining p∗=T(u)/up^*=T(u)/up∗=T(u)/u converts an expected amount to threshold units; p∗p^*p∗ can exceed one and is then certainly not a probability. Even when it lies below one, it is not the probability of the original event.

A mean-preserving change can move probability and severity oppositely¶

Let ZZZ be standard normal, m>0m>0m>0, and s>0s>0s>0. Define a lognormal loss with fixed mean mmm by

Distributional uncertainty needs a specified mixture¶

Jensen’s inequality applies to a particular function of a particular random parameter. It does not imply that every uncertainty change increases every payoff discrepancy. Here is one precise case, proved directly. Fix u≥xm>0u\ge x_m>0u≥xm​>0 and let A>1A>1A>1 be a random Pareto exponent. Conditional on A=aA=aA=a, use the Type-I law with that exponent and the same xmx_mxm​. Let c(a)c(a)c(a) denote its excess mean, and ℓ=log⁡(u/xm)≥0\ell=\log(u/x_m)\ge0ℓ=log(u/xm​)≥0.

c(a)=ue−aℓa−1,c′′(a)c(a)=(ℓ+1a−1)2+1(a−1)2>0.\begin{gathered} c(a)=\frac{u e^{-a\ell}}{a-1},\\ \begin{aligned} \frac{c''(a)}{c(a)}&=\left(\ell+\frac1{a-1}\right)^2\\ &\quad+\frac1{(a-1)^2}>0. \end{aligned} \end{gathered}c(a)=a−1ue−aℓ​,c(a)c′′(a)​​=(ℓ+a−11​)2+(a−1)21​>0.​​
(6)

Logarithmic differentiation proves the second identity. Tonelli gives the mixture excess mean E[c(A)]\mathbb E[c(A)]E[c(A)]. If EA<∞\mathbb EA<\inftyEA<∞, convexity therefore gives E[c(A)]≥c(EA)\mathbb E[c(A)]\ge c(\mathbb EA)E[c(A)]≥c(EA), allowing an infinite left side. A finite mean exponent above one does not ensure a finite mixture payoff: take AAA uniform on (1,2)(1,2)(1,2) and u=xm=1u=x_m=1u=xm​=1; then E[1/(A−1)]=∞\mathbb E[1/(A-1)]=\inftyE[1/(A−1)]=∞.

The mixture probability is E[e−Aℓ]\mathbb E[e^{-A\ell}]E[e−Aℓ]. Consequently the ratio T(u)/(upu)T(u)/(up_u)T(u)/(upu​) in the mixture weights each exponent by its probability of exceeding uuu. It is generally neither the ratio evaluated at EA\mathbb EAEA nor the unweighted average of the conditional ratios. This distinction prevents a Jensen comparison of one quantity from being silently transferred to another. The mixture-uncertainty note treats other parameter choices and moment gates.

Calibration and the Brier score¶

Let Q∈[0,1]Q\in[0,1]Q∈[0,1] be the announced probability and D∈{0,1}D\in\{0,1\}D∈{0,1} the outcome. Here QQQ is local forecast notation, not a probability measure. Calibration means E[D∣Q]=Q\mathbb E[D\mid Q]=QE[D∣Q]=Q almost surely. It concerns conditional frequencies among comparable forecasts. A constant forecast Q=pQ=pQ=p is calibrated whenever P(D=1)=pP(D=1)=pP(D=1)=p, even if it cannot distinguish high-risk from low-risk cases.

Write m(Q)=E[D∣Q]m(Q)=\mathbb E[D\mid Q]m(Q)=E[D∣Q] and R=(Q−D)2R=(Q-D)^2R=(Q−D)2 for one Brier score. We use the binary convention on [0,1][0,1][0,1], without the factor of two that arises from summing the errors for both outcome categories. Expansion of the square proves

E[R∣Q]=m(Q){1−m(Q)}+{Q−m(Q)}2.\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[R\mid Q] &=m(Q)\{1-m(Q)\}\\ &\quad+\{Q-m(Q)\}^2. \end{aligned}E[R∣Q]​=m(Q){1−m(Q)}+{Q−m(Q)}2.​
(7)

For a fixed conditional event probability, the expected score is uniquely minimized by reporting that probability. This is strict propriety; it does not mean that each finite sample rewards the truthful report, or that calibration identifies a severity distribution. Among calibrated forecasts, ER=E[Q(1−Q)]\mathbb E R=\mathbb E[Q(1-Q)]ER=E[Q(1−Q)]: more informative forecasts can have lower scores than the calibrated constant forecast.

For n≥1n\ge1n≥1 pairs define Bn=n−1∑iRiB_n=n^{-1}\sum_i R_iBn​=n−1∑i​Ri​. Pointwise, 0≤Bn≤10\le B_n\le10≤Bn​≤1, with no independence assumption. Every positive moment and every real exponential moment is finite. Boundedness does not imply small standardized kurtosis or fast averaging under dependence. For example, if every DiD_iDi​ equals the same Bernoulli variable and every Qi=0Q_i=0Qi​=0, then Bn=D1B_n=D_1Bn​=D1​ for all nnn; its variance never decreases.

Exact tally and fixed-forecast score laws¶

Now assume D1,…,DnD_1,\ldots,D_nD1​,…,Dn​ are iid Bernoulli with parameter ppp. The tally Kn=∑iDiK_n=\sum_iD_iKn​=∑i​Di​ counts occurrences, or correct predictions if DiD_iDi​ was defined as correctness. This law is independent of the magnitude distribution used to define each event, once ppp is fixed. Unequal event probabilities give a different law, and dependent indicators need their joint law. Differentiating nlog⁡(1−p+pet/n)n\log(1-p+pe^{t/n})nlog(1−p+pet/n) gives the third and fourth cumulants of the fraction, p(1−p)(1−2p)/n2p(1-p)(1-2p)/n^2p(1−p)(1−2p)/n2 and p(1−p){1−6p(1−p)}/n3p(1-p)\{1-6p(1-p)\}/n^3p(1−p){1−6p(1−p)}/n3. For 0<p<10<p<10<p<1, its excess kurtosis is {1−6p(1−p)}/{np(1−p)}\{1-6p(1-p)\}/\{np(1-p)\}{1−6p(1−p)}/{np(1−p)}. At p=0p=0p=0 or 1 the fraction is constant and standardized kurtosis is undefined.

An uncentered fraction converges to a point mass. An n−1n^{-1}n−1 decay of excess kurtosis is not a general n−1n^{-1}n−1 bound on normal-approximation error. See also the limit-law note.

If every forecast equals the same q∈[0,1]q\in[0,1]q∈[0,1], expand each score to obtain

At q=1/2q=1/2q=1/2, Bn=1/4B_n=1/4Bn​=1/4 for every outcome sequence, including sequences from a miscalibrated model. Random forecasts: two distinct beta experiments¶

Suppose QQQ has beta density fa,bf_{a,b}fa,b​ on (0,1)(0,1)(0,1), with shapes a,b>0a,b>0a,b>0. A beta marginal does not specify how DDD depends on QQQ. Replacing the conditional expectation by g(E[X∣A])g(\mathbb E[X\mid A])g(E[X∣A]) requires additional assumptions and generally changes the answer. For convex ggg, conditional Jensen gives a weak inequality when the expectations are defined; strict inequality needs more than nonlinearity alone. An arbitrary measurable ggg has no fixed Jensen direction. Expectation of a finite integrable hinge sum is the corresponding sum of expectations. An infinite sum needs an interchange justification.

The canonical hinge and angle treatment proves the finite piecewise-linear representation and explains smoothing and extrapolation. It is the relevant link to chapter 11’s ML discussion. It does not assert exact finite representation of every nonlinear payoff. For sequential exposure, absorbing resource paths retain all prior crossings. Conditioning on survival changes a distribution; it does not generally leave the original payoff law unchanged.

Binary calibration remains useful for a specified event. Choosing a safer action need not require distorting its probability: decision costs and severity belong in the payoff or loss function. The point-forecast note derives what changes when we score amounts rather than event indicators.
