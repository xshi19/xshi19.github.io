Cauchy running statistics

A location without a mean¶

Let X1,X2,…X_1,X_2,\ldotsX1​,X2​,… be iid Cauchy variables with location μ\muμ and scale σ>0\sigma>0σ>0. Their density and characteristic function are

f(x)=σπ((x−μ)2+σ2),φ(t)=exp⁡(iμt−σ∣t∣).\begin{gathered} f(x)=\frac{\sigma}{\pi((x-\mu)^2+\sigma^2)},\\ \varphi(t)=\exp(i\mu t-\sigma|t|). \end{gathered}f(x)=π((x−μ)2+σ2)σ​,φ(t)=exp(iμt−σ∣t∣).​
(1)

The characteristic function is a cited identity, not proved here (Durrett (2019), §3.3.1, Example 3.3.16, p. 131). Tail integration establishes the moment boundary.

Let XXX have the Cauchy density f(x)=σ/[π((x−μ)2+σ2)]f(x)=\sigma/[\pi((x-\mu)^2+\sigma^2)]f(x)=σ/[π((x−μ)2+σ2)], where μ∈R\mu\in\mathbb Rμ∈R and σ>0\sigma>0σ>0. For every p>0p>0p>0, E∣X∣p<∞\mathbb E|X|^p<\inftyE∣X∣p<∞ if and only if p<1p<1p<1. On either tail, f(x)∼σ/(πx2)f(x)\sim\sigma/(\pi x^2)f(x)∼σ/(πx2), so the moment integral has the same convergence boundary as ∫1∞xp−2 dx\int_1^\infty x^{p-2}\,dx∫1∞​xp−2dx. Thus μ\muμ is a location and median, not an expectation. See the stable-law convention for the shared scale parameterization.

The average has exactly the same law¶

We prove this finite-sample identity from the stated characteristic function.

Proposition 2 (Cauchy averages retain their law and spread)

Let X1,X2,…X_1,X_2,\ldotsX1​,X2​,… be iid Cauchy variables with location μ∈R\mu\in\mathbb Rμ∈R and scale σ>0\sigma>0σ>0. For every integer n≥1n\ge1n≥1, Xˉn=n−1∑i=1nXi\bar X_n=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^nX_iXˉn​=n−1∑i=1n​Xi​ has the same law as X1X_1X1​, and for every ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0,

Remarks. This argument alone does not classify every possible pathwise limit. Successive running averages share observations and are not asserted to be independent.

By contrast, the indicator strong law establishes consistency of medians.

Proposition 3 (Strong consistency of Cauchy sample medians)

Let X1,X2,…X_1,X_2,\ldotsX1​,X2​,… be iid Cauchy variables with location μ∈R\mu\in\mathbb Rμ∈R and scale σ>0\sigma>0σ>0. For each nnn, let mnm_nmn​ be any sample median: at least half of X1,…,XnX_1,\ldots,X_nX1​,…,Xn​ are at or below mnm_nmn​ and at least half are at or above it. Then mn→μm_n\to\mumn​→μ almost surely.

Proof 3

For fixed ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0, the strong law applied to the indicators 1{Xi≤μ−ε}\mathbf1_{\{X_i\le\mu-\varepsilon\}}1{Xi​≤μ−ε}​ and 1{Xi≤μ+ε}\mathbf1_{\{X_i\le\mu+\varepsilon\}}1{Xi​≤μ+ε}​ eventually puts fewer than half the observations at or below the lower endpoint and more than half at or below the upper endpoint. Every sample median is then inside that interval, since F(μ−ε)<1/2<F(μ+ε)F(\mu-\varepsilon)<1/2<F(\mu+\varepsilon)F(μ−ε)<1/2<F(μ+ε). Intersect over rational positive ε\varepsilonε to obtain almost-sure convergence.

Remark. Median consistency does not restore the missing mean.

Paths and repeated samples¶

Use running_mean for one path and repeated_centered_sums for independent repetitions. Here centering at zero names the Cauchy location; it does not assert a population mean.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import cauchy
from xmath.incerto.limits import repeated_centered_sums

n = np.array([10, 100, 1000])
sums = repeated_centered_sums(cauchy(), n, repetitions=400, random_state=71)
for size, row in zip(n, sums / n[:, None]):
    q = np.quantile(row, [.1, .5, .9])
    print(f"n={size}: " + ", ".join(f"{v:.4g}" for v in q))

Output

n=10: -2.694, 0.01121, 2.404
n=100: -3.183, -0.05087, 3.134
n=1000: -3.107, 0.04288, 2.812

Higher moments and ergodicity¶

The moment boundary gives a direct application of the nonnegative infinite-mean theorem, which is proved in that note.

Corollary 1 (Divergence of averages of Cauchy absolute powers)

If X1,X2,…X_1,X_2,\ldotsX1​,X2​,… are iid Cauchy variables with location μ∈R\mu\in\mathbb Rμ∈R and scale σ>0\sigma>0σ>0, then for each fixed p≥1p\ge1p≥1,

1n∑i=1n∣Xi∣p⟶+∞almost surely.\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n|X_i|^p\longrightarrow+\infty \qquad\text{almost surely}.n1​i=1∑n​∣Xi​∣p⟶+∞almost surely.
(5)
Proof 4

By Proposition 1, E∣Xi∣p=∞\mathbb E|X_i|^p=\inftyE∣Xi​∣p=∞. The variables ∣Xi∣p|X_i|^p∣Xi​∣p are iid and nonnegative, so the cited infinite-mean theorem applies.

Remark. That theorem does not apply to the signed Cauchy observations themselves. A principal-value cancellation is not a Lebesgue expectation.

An iid Cauchy sequence is ergodic under the shift. The integrability hypothesis of the ergodic averaging theorem fails for the identity observable. Calling the process nonergodic because its sample mean fails would confuse two different conditions.
