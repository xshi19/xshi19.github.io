Dispersion Ratio Under Fat Tails

Statement¶

Let x1,…,xnx_1,\dots,x_nx1​,…,xn​ be observed data, with n≥1n\ge1n≥1 and sample mean xˉ\bar xxˉ. and

For Gaussian data, both are calibrated to estimate the same σ\sigmaσ as the standard deviation. Under fat-tailed or contaminated data, the ratios

Std⁡MeanAD⁡,Std⁡MedianAD⁡\frac{\operatorname{Std}}{\operatorname{MeanAD}}, \qquad \frac{\operatorname{Std}}{\operatorname{MedianAD}}MeanADStd​,MedianADStd​
(3)

can rise because sample standard deviation is more sensitive to isolated extremes. The mean absolute deviation uses all observations; the median absolute deviation depends on their central order statistics.

The observed values x1,…,xnx_1,\dots,x_nx1​,…,xn​, scale σ\sigmaσ, standard normal CDF Φ\PhiΦ, and expectation notation follow the shared notation table. The names MeanAD and MedianAD are local conventions for these two different calibrated scales.

Examples¶

  • For Gaussian data, the standard deviation, normal-scaled mean absolute deviation, and normal-scaled median absolute deviation target the same σ\sigmaσ, so the ratios should be near 1.

  • For Student-t or Pareto samples, large observations can push the standard deviation much higher than the absolute-deviation scales.

Use dispersion_ratios.

from xmath.incerto.stats import dispersion_ratios

for sample in ([2, 2, 2], [0, 0, 10], [1, 2, 3]):
    mean_ratio, median_ratio = dispersion_ratios(sample)
    print(f"{sample}: Std/MeanAD={mean_ratio:.4g}, Std/MedianAD={median_ratio:.4g}")

Output

Calibration¶

We justify the Gaussian scale constants here; these identities do not establish a tail class. Integrating the standard normal density on the positive half-line gives E∣Z∣=2∫0∞zϕ(z) dz=2/π\mathbb E|Z|=2\int_0^\infty z\phi(z)\,dz=\sqrt{2/\pi}E∣Z∣=2∫0∞​zϕ(z)dz=2/π​. E∣X−μ∣=σ2π.\mathbb E|X-\mu| = \sigma\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}.E∣X−μ∣=σπ2​​.
(4)

Thus multiplying the mean absolute deviation by π/2\sqrt{\pi/2}π/2​ calibrates it to σ\sigmaσ under a normal model. Also,

median⁡(∣X−μ∣)=σΦ−1(0.75),\operatorname{median}(|X-\mu|) = \sigma\Phi^{-1}(0.75),median(∣X−μ∣)=σΦ−1(0.75),

so dividing the raw median absolute deviation by Φ−1(0.75)\Phi^{-1}(0.75)Φ−1(0.75) gives the same normal calibration.

These identities are normal-model calibrations, not tail theorems. If the sample has extreme observations, the standard deviation’s quadratic loss gives those observations much more weight than either absolute-deviation statistic.

Simulation¶

The simulation compares Gaussian, Student-t, and Pareto samples across sample sizes. It reports repeated-simulation medians of the two dispersion ratios.

Use dispersion_ratios.

import numpy as np
from xmath.incerto.stats import dispersion_ratios

rng = np.random.default_rng(20261020)
for label, sample in (("Normal", rng.normal(size=5000)),
                      ("Student 3", rng.standard_t(3, size=5000))):
    values = dispersion_ratios(sample)
    print(f"{label}: [" + ", ".join(f"{v:.3f}" for v in values) + "]")

Output

Normal: [1.003, 1.021]
Student 3: [1.175, 1.383]

These are sample diagnostics, not estimates of a tail exponent.

Figure regeneration: uv run python demos/incerto/dispersion_ratio.py.

The Gaussian calibration explains why the simulated Gaussian ratios are near one. The Student-t and Pareto ratios are larger because the standard deviation is more sensitive to extremes than the absolute-deviation scales. The ratio is a stress signal, not a fitted tail exponent. The moment-estimation note explains why a finite population moment can still have an estimator with infinite variance.

Caveats¶

  • A high ratio is a diagnostic, not a fitted tail exponent.

  • The mean absolute deviation and median absolute deviation are scaled here to match a Gaussian standard deviation. Other conventions use unscaled values.

  • Median absolute deviation can be too robust for payoff questions where a rare outlier dominates the quantity of interest.

  • In an infinite-variance sample, the ratios are sample-path objects; the population variance does not exist.

  • Dependence and volatility clustering can move these ratios even when the one-step marginal distribution is unchanged.
