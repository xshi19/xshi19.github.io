Time averages and ensemble averages

Which observable is averaged?¶

For a stationary process (Xk)(X_k)(Xk​), an ensemble mean is E[g(X0)]\mathbb E[g(X_0)]E[g(X0​)]; a time average is n−1∑k=0n−1g(Xk)n^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(X_k)n−1∑k=0n−1​g(Xk​) along one realization. The observable ggg and its integrability are part of the question. Stationarity means that shifting every time index preserves joint laws. Ergodicity means that events invariant under that shift have probability zero or one.

For an integrable observable of a stationary process, Birkhoff’s theorem gives the almost-sure limit E[g(X0)∣I]\mathbb E[g(X_0)\mid\mathcal I]E[g(X0​)∣I], where I\mathcal II is the invariant sigma-field. Under ergodicity this is the constant E[g(X0)]\mathbb E[g(X_0)]E[g(X0​)]. This general theorem is cited, not proved: Durrett (2019), Theorem 6.2.1, p. 335. The following finite examples are proved directly.

For a stationary nonergodic example, draw B∈{0,1}B\in\{0,1\}B∈{0,1} once with equal probabilities and put Xk=BX_k=BXk​=B at every time. The time average is BBB, whereas the ensemble mean is 1/21/21/2. The event {B=1}\{B=1\}{B=1} is invariant and has probability 1/21/21/2. Independence fails even though all moments exist.

Multiplicative growth: two different targets¶

Let A1,A2,…A_1,A_2,\ldotsA1​,A2​,… be iid positive multipliers with E∣log⁡A1∣<∞\mathbb E|\log A_1|<\inftyE∣logA1​∣<∞ and EA1<∞\mathbb E A_1<\inftyEA1​<∞. For initial wealth w0>0w_0>0w0​>0, define Wn=w0∏i=1nAiW_n=w_0\prod_{i=1}^n A_iWn​=w0​∏i=1n​Ai​. Independence and the strong law give, respectively,

EWn=w0(EA1)n,1nlog⁡(Wn/w0)→a.s.Elog⁡A1.\begin{gathered} \mathbb E W_n=w_0(\mathbb E A_1)^n,\\ \frac1n\log(W_n/w_0)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathbb E\log A_1. \end{gathered}EWn​=w0​(EA1​)n,n1​log(Wn​/w0​)a.s.​ElogA1​.​
(1)

The first formula follows by factoring the expectation of a finite product. The second follows by taking logarithms and averaging integrable iid increments. Concavity of log gives Elog⁡A1≤log⁡EA1\mathbb E\log A_1\le\log\mathbb E A_1ElogA1​≤logEA1​, strictly for a nonconstant positive multiplier. These statements concern expected wealth and realized log growth. They are not two competing values for the same time average. Wealth itself generally is not stationary, so the stationary ergodic theorem cannot be applied to WnW_nWn​ without additional structure.

Take Ai=1.8A_i=1.8Ai​=1.8 or 0.6, each with probability 1/21/21/2. Then

EAi=1.2,Elog⁡Ai=12log⁡(1.08)>0.\begin{gathered} \mathbb E A_i=1.2,\\ \mathbb E\log A_i=\tfrac12\log(1.08)>0. \end{gathered}EAi​=1.2,ElogAi​=21​log(1.08)>0.​
(2)

Both targets grow, at different exponential rates. For the equally likely pair 1.5,0.61.5,0.61.5,0.6, expected wealth grows at rate log⁡(1.05)>0\log(1.05)>0log(1.05)>0, while the path log-growth rate is 12log⁡(0.9)<0\tfrac12\log(0.9)<021​log(0.9)<0, so Wn→0W_n\to0Wn​→0 almost surely. A positive expected payoff therefore does not determine long-run multiplicative growth. Choosing expected utility, expected wealth or growth as an objective is a further decision, not an ergodic theorem.

finite_payoff_mean computes both finite expectations; running_mean can average realized log increments.

print(f"Mean log multiplier: {finite_payoff_mean([.5, .5], np.log(a)):.4g}")

A thin-tailed rate can produce a power-tailed outcome¶

For a fixed horizon T>0T>0T>0, initial size xm>0x_m>0xm​>0, and a random constant rate R∼Exp(λ)R\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)R∼Exp(λ), let X=xmeTRX=x_m e^{TR}X=xm​eTR. For x≥xmx\ge x_mx≥xm​,

P(X>x)=P ⁣(R>log⁡(x/xm)T)=(xmx)λ/T.\begin{aligned} P(X>x)&=P\!\left(R>\frac{\log(x/x_m)}T\right)\\ &=\left(\frac{x_m}{x}\right)^{\lambda/T}. \end{aligned}P(X>x)​=P(R>Tlog(x/xm​)​)=(xxm​​)λ/T.​
(3)

Thus the outcome is exactly Pareto with α=λ/T\alpha=\lambda/Tα=λ/T, despite the exponential rate having all power moments. Integrating the exponential density gives, for p>0p>0p>0,

EXp={xmpλ/(λ−pT),pT<λ,∞,pT≥λ.\mathbb EX^p= \begin{cases} x_m^p\lambda/(\lambda-pT),&pT<\lambda,\\ \infty,&pT\ge\lambda. \end{cases}EXp={xmp​λ/(λ−pT),∞,​pT<λ,pT≥λ.​
(4)

This is a change of variables at a fixed horizon. It does not establish stationarity, ergodicity, or a model of epidemics. Specifying a right tail alone cannot determine a two-sided rate density. More general mixing assumptions are considered in parameter uncertainty.
