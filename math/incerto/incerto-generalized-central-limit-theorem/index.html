Generalized Central Limit Theorem

Statement¶

The ordinary central limit theorem uses finite variance and normalizes sums by n\sqrt{n}n​. The substantive generalized central limit theorem is a classification theorem: a nondegenerate distribution can occur as a weak limit of centered and normalized iid sums if and only if it is stable. In other words, if there are constants an>0a_n>0an​>0 and bnb_nbn​ such that

X1+⋯+Xn−bnan⇒Z,\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n-b_n}{a_n} \Rightarrow Z,an​X1​+⋯+Xn​−bn​​⇒Z,
(1)

for a nondegenerate limit ZZZ, then ZZZ must be stable; conversely, stable laws arise as such limits for suitable iid summands. The Gaussian law is the stable case α=2\alpha=2α=2. The heavy-tailed stable cases have index 0<α<20<\alpha<20<α<2.

For a two-sided regularly varying sufficient condition, assume

P(∣X∣>x)=x−αL(x),0<α<2,\begin{gathered} \mathbb P(|X|>x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x),\\ 0<\alpha<2, \end{gathered}P(∣X∣>x)=x−αL(x),0<α<2,​
(2)

where LLL is slowly varying at infinity, with tail balance

P(X>x)P(∣X∣>x)→p,P(X<−x)P(∣X∣>x)→1−p,0≤p≤1.\begin{gathered} \frac{\mathbb P(X>x)}{\mathbb P(|X|>x)}\to p,\\ \frac{\mathbb P(X<-x)}{\mathbb P(|X|>x)}\to 1-p,\\ 0\le p\le1. \end{gathered}P(∣X∣>x)P(X>x)​→p,P(∣X∣>x)P(X<−x)​→1−p,0≤p≤1.​
(3)

Choose ana_nan​ so that nP(∣X∣>an)→1n\mathbb P(|X|>a_n)\to1nP(∣X∣>an​)→1. Then, with suitable centering constants bnb_nbn​,

X1+⋯+Xn−bnan⇒Zα,\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n-b_n}{a_n} \Rightarrow Z_\alpha,an​X1​+⋯+Xn​−bn​​⇒Zα​,
(4)

where ZαZ_\alphaZα​ is an α\alphaα-stable law whose skewness is determined by 2p−12p-12p−1. For nonnegative Pareto-type examples this reduces to a strongly right-skewed stable limit, and the scaling is of order n1/αn^{1/\alpha}n1/α up to a slowly varying factor. When 1<α<21<\alpha<21<α<2, the mean exists but the variance is infinite, so a common centering choice is bn=nE[X]b_n=n\mathbb E[X]bn​=nE[X].

A common centering summary is

bn={0,0<α<1,nE[X1{∣X∣≤an}],α=1,nE[X],1<α<2.\begin{gathered} b_n=\\ \begin{cases} 0, & 0<\alpha<1,\\ n\mathbb E[X\mathbf 1_{\{|X|\le a_n\}}], & \alpha=1,\\ n\mathbb E[X], & 1<\alpha<2. \end{cases} \end{gathered}bn​=⎩⎨⎧​0,nE[X1{∣X∣≤an​}​],nE[X],​0<α<1,α=1,1<α<2.​​
(5)

The first row applies in the usual nonnegative case; the second is a standard choice at α=1\alpha=1α=1.

Here Sn=∑i=1nXiS_n=\sum_{i=1}^n X_iSn​=∑i=1n​Xi​ is the sum, Mn=max⁡1≤i≤nXiM_n=\max_{1\le i\le n}X_iMn​=max1≤i≤n​Xi​ is the maximum, E\mathbb EE is expectation, and ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ denotes convergence in distribution. The tail exponent α\alphaα, norming constants ana_nan​, centering constants bnb_nbn​, right-tail weight ppp (left-tail weight 1−p1-p1−p), and stable limit ZαZ_\alphaZα​ are defined above. See Shared notation.

We record the stable-limit contrast needed by the site’s heavy-tail pages. We do not classify every possible domain of attraction or every parameterization of stable laws.

The classification and tail condition are cited from Durrett (2019), Theorems 3.8.8 (p. 191) and 3.8.2 (p. 186), with characteristic functions in §3.8, p. 189. That theorem uses truncated centering; the conventions above can change the limit’s location. For exact Pareto with 1<α<21<\alpha<21<α<2 and an=xmn1/αa_n=x_m n^{1/\alpha}an​=xm​n1/α, the difference between full and truncated centering divided by ana_nan​ is the constant α/(α−1)\alpha/(\alpha-1)α/(α−1), since nE[X1{X>an}]/an=α/(α−1)n\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_{\{X>a_n\}}]/a_n=\alpha/(\alpha-1)nE[X1{X>an​}​]/an​=α/(α−1). For a scale satisfying only nP(X>an)→1nP(X>a_n)\to1nP(X>an​)→1, this ratio converges to the same constant. Thus full-mean centering preserves convergence, with the limit shifted downward by α/(α−1)\alpha/(\alpha-1)α/(α−1). These theorems are not proved by the maximum calculation below.

Why n\sqrt{n}n​ fails¶

The normal law is not the only possible attractor for sums. It is the finite-variance attractor. For the regularly varying tails with 0<α<20<\alpha<20<α<2 considered here, the largest observations remain visible at the scale of the centered sum, and n\sqrt{n}n​ is no longer the right normalization.

For a Pareto-type tail with exponent α<2\alpha<2α<2, the natural scale of the maximum is about n1/αn^{1/\alpha}n1/α. Stable normalization puts the centered sum on that same order. This is why infinite-variance sums can keep producing large jumps instead of smoothing into Gaussian-looking noise.

Scaling argument¶

The full generalized central limit theorem is a stable-law classification result, so we cite it rather than reproduce the proof. What we prove directly is the exact Pareto maximum scaling calculation, which shows why the usual n\sqrt nn​ scale is too small when α<2\alpha<2α<2.

For a Pareto Type I variable with survival Fˉ(x)=(xm/x)α\bar F(x)=(x_m/x)^\alphaFˉ(x)=(xm​/x)α, choose an=xmn1/αa_n=x_m n^{1/\alpha}an​=xm​n1/α. Then for y>0y>0y>0 and sufficiently large nnn such that any≥xma_n y\ge x_man​y≥xm​,

P(Mn/an≤y)=(1−1nyα)n→e−y−α.\begin{aligned} &\mathbb P(M_n/a_n\le y)\\ &\quad=\left(1-\frac{1}{ny^\alpha}\right)^n\\ &\quad\to e^{-y^{-\alpha}}. \end{aligned}​P(Mn​/an​≤y)=(1−nyα1​)n→e−y−α.​
(6)

The maximum remains of order ana_nan​. Since ana_nan​ grows faster than n\sqrt{n}n​ when α<2\alpha<2α<2, the Gaussian finite-variance scaling is not the right asymptotic scale for such tails. This maximum calculation identifies the right order of extreme observations; it is a scaling heuristic, not a proof of stable convergence for sums.

Pareto scaling comparison¶

Take exact Pareto Type I with xm=1x_m=1xm​=1 and α=1.5\alpha=1.5α=1.5. Its mean is 3 and its variance is infinite. With Sn=∑i=1nXiS_n=\sum_{i=1}^n X_iSn​=∑i=1n​Xi​, compare

Tn=Sn−3nn2/3,Gn=Sn−3nn=n1/6Tn.\begin{gathered} T_n=\frac{S_n-3n}{n^{2/3}},\\ G_n=\frac{S_n-3n}{\sqrt n}=n^{1/6}T_n. \end{gathered}Tn​=n2/3Sn​−3n​,Gn​=n​Sn​−3n​=n1/6Tn​.​
(7)

The identity holds for every sample. Consequently, whenever its interquartile range is nonzero, the spread of GnG_nGn​ is exactly n1/6n^{1/6}n1/6 times that of TnT_nTn​. Here are the normalization factors, rounded to three decimals:

The cited stable-limit theorem implies convergence of the central quantiles of TnT_nTn​ to those of a nondegenerate continuous stable law. Exact stable sums and their averages¶

We use a symmetric stable law with characteristic function

EeitX=exp⁡(iμt−∣σt∣α),0<α≤2,σ>0.\begin{gathered} \mathbb E e^{itX}=\exp(i\mu t-|\sigma t|^\alpha),\\ 0<\alpha\le2,\quad\sigma>0. \end{gathered}EeitX=exp(iμt−∣σt∣α),0<α≤2,σ>0.​
(8)

Here α\alphaα is the stability index; for α<2\alpha<2α<2 it is also the power-tail exponent. At α=2\alpha=2α=2 this is N(μ,2σ2)N(\mu,2\sigma^2)N(μ,2σ2), so σ\sigmaσ is not its standard deviation. The existence of these stable laws is a cited input from Durrett (2019), Example 3.3.23, pp. 138–139 (with the Gaussian endpoint immediate). Multiplying characteristic functions proves, for iid copies,

Sn−nμ=dn1/α(X1−μ),Xˉn−μ=dn1/α−1(X1−μ).\begin{gathered} S_n-n\mu\overset d=n^{1/\alpha}(X_1-\mu),\\ \bar X_n-\mu\overset d=n^{1/\alpha-1}(X_1-\mu). \end{gathered}Sn​−nμ=dn1/α(X1​−μ),Xˉn​−μ=dn1/α−1(X1​−μ).​
(9)

For 1<α≤21<\alpha\le21<α≤2 the scale shrinks. The finite absolute moment proved below also makes the strong law applicable, so Xˉn→μ\bar X_n\to\muXˉn​→μ almost surely. At α=1\alpha=1α=1 the average keeps its Cauchy law; at α<1\alpha<1α<1 the scale grows and μ\muμ is only a location. These exact identities must not be transferred to a Pareto parent merely because its normalized sums have a stable limit.

Mean absolute deviation of a symmetric stable law¶

For the preceding parameterization and 1<α≤21<\alpha\le21<α≤2, we prove

E∣X−μ∣=2σπΓ(1−1/α),E∣Xˉn−μ∣=2σπΓ(1−1/α)n1/α−1.\begin{gathered} \mathbb E|X-\mu|=\frac{2\sigma}{\pi}\Gamma(1-1/\alpha),\\ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E|\bar X_n-\mu|\\ &\quad=\frac{2\sigma}{\pi}\Gamma(1-1/\alpha)n^{1/\alpha-1}. \end{aligned} \end{gathered}E∣X−μ∣=π2σ​Γ(1−1/α),​E∣Xˉn​−μ∣=π2σ​Γ(1−1/α)n1/α−1.​​
(10)

Start with ∣x∣=(2/π)∫0∞(1−cos⁡(tx))/t2 dt|x|=(2/\pi)\int_0^\infty(1-\cos(tx))/t^2\,dt∣x∣=(2/π)∫0∞​(1−cos(tx))/t2dt. For completeness, the integral at x=1x=1x=1 equals π/2\pi/2π/2: write u−2=∫0∞se−su dsu^{-2}=\int_0^\infty s e^{-su}\,dsu−2=∫0∞​se−suds and use Tonelli to get ∫0∞(1+s2)−1 ds=π/2\int_0^\infty (1+s^2)^{-1}\,ds=\pi/2∫0∞​(1+s2)−1ds=π/2. Scaling gives the identity for all xxx. Apply Tonelli again and change the integration coordinate via t←(σt)αt\leftarrow(\sigma t)^\alphat←(σt)α. In the remaining integral and its analysis, ttt denotes this transformed coordinate:

E∣X−μ∣=2σπα∫0∞(1−e−t)t−1−1/α dt.\begin{aligned} &\mathbb E|X-\mu|\\ &\quad=\frac{2\sigma}{\pi\alpha} \int_0^\infty(1-e^{-t})t^{-1-1/\alpha}\,dt. \end{aligned}​E∣X−μ∣=πα2σ​∫0∞​(1−e−t)t−1−1/αdt.​
(11)

Integration by parts makes the integral αΓ(1−1/α)\alpha\Gamma(1-1/\alpha)αΓ(1−1/α); both boundary terms vanish when α>1\alpha>1α>1. Cumulants measure particular aspects of convergence¶

Let the iid summands have mean μ\muμ, variance 0<σ2<∞0<\sigma^2<\infty0<σ2<∞, and finite absolute moment of integer order r≥3r\ge3r≥3. With characteristic function φX(t)=EeitX\varphi_X(t)=\mathbb E e^{itX}φX​(t)=EeitX, define the local rrrth cumulant by κr(X)=i−r(log⁡φX)(r)(0)\kappa_r(X)=i^{-r}(\log\varphi_X)^{(r)}(0)κr​(X)=i−r(logφX​)(r)(0), using the continuous logarithm near φX(0)=1\varphi_X(0)=1φX​(0)=1. Standardize Sn←(Sn−nμ)/(σn)S_n\leftarrow(S_n-n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt n)Sn​←(Sn​−nμ)/(σn​); throughout this section, SnS_nSn​ denotes the standardized sum. Multiplication of characteristic functions and the chain rule prove

κr(Sn)=n1−r/2κr(X)σr.\kappa_r(S_n)=n^{1-r/2}\frac{\kappa_r(X)}{\sigma^r}.κr​(Sn​)=n1−r/2σrκr​(X)​.
(12)

Thus standardized skewness decays as n−1/2n^{-1/2}n−1/2 when the third absolute moment is finite, and excess kurtosis as n−1n^{-1}n−1 when the fourth moment is finite. Direct integration gives zero skewness and excess −6/5-6/5−6/5 for U(0,1)U(0,1)U(0,1); differentiating −log⁡(1−t)-\log(1-t)−log(1−t) gives skewness 2 and excess 6 for Exp(1)\mathrm{Exp}(1)Exp(1). For iid sums, divide these excesses by nnn. These calculations describe moments, not a distance between CDFs. Zero excess kurtosis does not identify a Gaussian law: a variable taking 0,3,−30,\sqrt3,-\sqrt30,3​,−3​ with probabilities 2/3,1/6,1/62/3,1/6,1/62/3,1/6,1/6 has variance one and fourth moment three, but is discrete.

For a CDF error bound, the cited Berry–Esseen theorem (Durrett (2019), Theorem 3.4.17, p. 157) requires ρ=E∣X−μ∣3<∞\rho=\mathbb E|X-\mu|^3<\inftyρ=E∣X−μ∣3<∞ and gives sup⁡x∣P(Sn≤x)−Φ(x)∣≤3ρ/(σ3n)\sup_x|P(S_n\le x)-\Phi(x)|\le3\rho/(\sigma^3\sqrt n)supx​∣P(Sn​≤x)−Φ(x)∣≤3ρ/(σ3n​). The constant is sufficient, not optimal. For U(0,1)U(0,1)U(0,1), ρ/σ3=33/4\rho/\sigma^3=3\sqrt3/4ρ/σ3=33​/4; for Exp(1)\mathrm{Exp}(1)Exp(1) it is 12/e−212/e-212/e−2, obtained by splitting the centered absolute-moment integral at one. These upper bounds do not prove a universal ranking of convergence speeds.

For exact Pareto, the moment gate requires α>2\alpha>2α>2 for finite variance, α>3\alpha>3α>3 for Berry–Esseen’s third absolute moment, and α>4\alpha>4α>4 for excess kurtosis. The source’s α=2\alpha=2α=2 example has infinite variance. Its Gaussian-domain scaling requires an additional logarithmic factor; it is not a finite-variance n\sqrt nn​ example. For 1<α<21<\alpha<21<α<2, the non-Gaussian limit above applies. The Lyapunov and Lindeberg extensions in SCoFT §7.5 need their own array/moment conditions; the iid examples here do not prove those theorems.

Caveats¶

  • Stable-law parameterizations vary across books and software. The exact symmetric calculations specify their characteristic function; the general attraction statement does not fix a software skew/location convention.

  • The displayed regular-variation and tail-balance condition covers the non-Gaussian stable range 0<α<20<\alpha<20<α<2; the Gaussian boundary needs a different criterion.

  • Right-tail regular variation alone is not enough for a general two-sided variable; the left tail can change the scale or skewness of the limit.

  • Some infinite-variance distributions can still be in the Gaussian domain of attraction when their truncated second moment is slowly varying.

  • For 0<α<10<\alpha<10<α<1, the absolute first moment is infinite. At α=1\alpha=1α=1, its finiteness depends on the slowly varying factor: the tail-integral criterion is ∫∞L(x)/x dx<∞\int^\infty L(x)/x\,dx<\infty∫∞L(x)/xdx<∞. For example, a nonnegative variable with survival 1/[x(log⁡x)2]1/[x(\log x)^2]1/[x(logx)2] for x≥ex\ge ex≥e has a finite mean, since that tail integral equals 1. Exact Pareto tails have infinite mean at α=1\alpha=1α=1. See the boundary discussion in Karamata’s theorem. The truncated centering above remains a standard choice at this boundary; finite mean alone does not justify replacing it by nE[X]n\mathbb E[X]nE[X] without checking the resulting location shift on the ana_nan​ scale.

  • Finite samples can look calmer than the asymptotic theory suggests until a large observation arrives.
