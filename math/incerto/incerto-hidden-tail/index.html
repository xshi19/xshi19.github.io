The tail beyond the sample maximum

A sample has a largest observation; an unbounded population has no largest possible outcome. The probability beyond that observation can be small while its contribution to a moment remains substantial. We derive both quantities under iid sampling, then separate them from empirical CDF accuracy. SCoFT v4 §§9.2–9.3, pp. 177–181, motivates the comparison Taleb, 2025. The calculations below use exact finite-sample laws.

Missing probability¶

Let X1,…,XnX_1,\ldots,X_nX1​,…,Xn​ be iid from a continuous CDF FFF, let Mn=max⁡iXiM_n=\max_iX_iMn​=maxi​Xi​, and write Fˉ=1−F\bar F=1-FFˉ=1−F. Define the random missing mass Un=Fˉ(Mn)U_n=\bar F(M_n)Un​=Fˉ(Mn​). The probability integral transform makes the F(Xi)F(X_i)F(Xi​) independent uniform variables, so, for 0≤u≤10\le u\le10≤u≤1,

P(Un>u)=(1−u)n,Un∼Beta⁡(1,n),E[Un]=1n+1.\begin{aligned} P(U_n>u)&=(1-u)^n,\\ U_n&\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1,n),\\ \mathbb E[U_n]&=\frac1{n+1}. \end{aligned}P(Un​>u)Un​E[Un​]​=(1−u)n,∼Beta(1,n),=n+11​.​
(1)

The first identity follows by requiring every F(Xi)F(X_i)F(Xi​) to be below 1−u1-u1−u; integrating it from zero to one gives the expectation. It also proves that an independent next draw sets a new upper record with probability 1/(n+1)1/(n+1)1/(n+1). This result concerns probability, regardless of the sizes of the outcomes. Continuity excludes ties; a discrete parent needs a different calculation.

For fixed t≥0t\ge0t≥0, eventually t<nt<nt<n and

P(nUn>t)=(1−t/n)n⟶e−t.P(nU_n>t)=(1-t/n)^n\longrightarrow e^{-t}.P(nUn​>t)=(1−t/n)n⟶e−t.
(2)

Thus nUnnU_nnUn​ converges to an exponential law with rate one. An exponential law for the unscaled missing mass is an approximation, not its exact finite-nnn distribution; the latter is supported on [0,1][0,1][0,1].

Hidden Pareto moments¶

Now assume the entire parent is Pareto Type I, with lower cutoff xm>0x_m>0xm​>0 and exponent α>0\alpha>0α>0. For a nonnegative order ppp and fixed t≥xmt\ge x_mt≥xm​, define the unconditional tail contribution

Tp(t)=E[Xp1{X>t}].T_p(t)=\mathbb E[X^p\mathbf1_{\{X>t\}}].Tp​(t)=E[Xp1{X>t}​].
(3)

When p<αp<\alphap<α, integrating the Pareto density gives

Tp(t)=∫t∞αxmαxp−α−1 dx=αxmpα−p(xmt)α−p.\begin{aligned} T_p(t)&=\int_t^\infty\alpha x_m^\alpha x^{p-\alpha-1}\,dx\\ &=\frac{\alpha x_m^p}{\alpha-p} \left(\frac{x_m}{t}\right)^{\alpha-p}. \end{aligned}Tp​(t)​=∫t∞​αxmα​xp−α−1dx=α−pαxmp​​(txm​​)α−p.​
(4)

For p≥αp\ge\alphap≥α this integral diverges for every finite ttt; the population moment is infinite. There is then no finite moment fraction to report. For p<αp<\alphap<α, let r=1−p/α>0r=1-p/\alpha>0r=1−p/α>0 and define the random fraction

Rn,p=Tp(Mn)E[Xp]=Unr.R_{n,p}=\frac{T_p(M_n)}{\mathbb E[X^p]}=U_n^r.Rn,p​=E[Xp]Tp​(Mn​)​=Unr​.
(5)

This uses a new population draw XXX independent of the sample defining MnM_nMn​. It does not assign unobserved values to that sample. Transforming the Beta law proves, for 0≤h≤10\le h\le10≤h≤1,

P(Rn,p≤h)=1−(1−h1/r)n.P(R_{n,p}\le h)=1-(1-h^{1/r})^n.P(Rn,p​≤h)=1−(1−h1/r)n.
(6)

Integrating against the Beta density gives the exact expected fraction, with BBB and Γ\GammaΓ denoting beta and gamma functions:

E[Rn,p]=nB(1+r,n)=Γ(n+1)Γ(1+r)Γ(n+1+r)∼Γ(1+r)n−r.\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[R_{n,p}]&=nB(1+r,n)\\ &=\frac{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(1+r)}{\Gamma(n+1+r)}\\ &\sim\Gamma(1+r)n^{-r}. \end{aligned}E[Rn,p​]​=nB(1+r,n)=Γ(n+1+r)Γ(n+1)Γ(1+r)​∼Γ(1+r)n−r.​
(7)

The last equivalence follows from Stirling’s gamma-ratio asymptotic for fixed rrr. For the mean, r=1−1/αr=1-1/\alphar=1−1/α and α>1\alpha>1α>1 is required. As α\alphaα approaches one, this decay exponent approaches zero. The probability fraction still has expectation 1/(n+1)1/(n+1)1/(n+1).

Comparison with a normal parent¶

For standard normal XXX, write ϕ\phiϕ for its density and Φ\PhiΦ for its CDF. Since ϕ′(x)=−xϕ(x)\phi'(x)=-x\phi(x)ϕ′(x)=−xϕ(x), direct integration gives, for every real ttt,

E[X1{X>t}]=ϕ(t).\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_{\{X>t\}}]=\phi(t).E[X1{X>t}​]=ϕ(t).
(8)

The exact maximum density is nϕ(t)Φ(t)n−1n\phi(t)\Phi(t)^{n-1}nϕ(t)Φ(t)n−1. Consequently,

E[T1(Mn)]=∫−∞∞nϕ(t)2Φ(t)n−1 dt.\begin{gathered} \mathbb E[T_1(M_n)]\\ =\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}n\phi(t)^2\Phi(t)^{n-1}\,dt. \end{gathered}E[T1​(Mn​)]=∫−∞∞​nϕ(t)2Φ(t)n−1dt.​
(9)

All maxima are included, even negative ones. This is a positive upper-tail contribution, not a fraction of the normal mean, which is zero. For a centered normal with standard deviation σ\sigmaσ, multiply this expression by σ\sigmaσ; dividing by σ\sigmaσ recovers the displayed quantity.

For stable numerical integration set W=−nlog⁡Φ(Mn)W=-n\log\Phi(M_n)W=−nlogΦ(Mn​). Its survival is e−we^{-w}e−w for w≥0w\ge0w≥0, since Φ(Mn)\Phi(M_n)Φ(Mn​) has CDF vnv^nvn. This is an exact change of variables; the helper’s quadrature is numerical and reports an estimated integration error.

What the empirical distribution estimates¶

The empirical probability measure P^n=n−1∑iδXi\widehat P_n=n^{-1}\sum_i\delta_{X_i}Pn​=n−1∑i​δXi​​ is a valid empirical distribution. Its strict survival is

Fˉ^n(u)=1n∑i1{Xi>u}.\widehat{\bar F}_n(u)=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbf1_{\{X_i>u\}}.Fˉn​(u)=n1​i∑​1{Xi​>u}​.
(10)

At a fixed, nonrandom uuu, independence makes the numerator binomial with success probability q=Fˉ(u)q=\bar F(u)q=Fˉ(u). The empirical survival fraction has expectation qqq and variance q(1−q)/nq(1-q)/nq(1−q)/n. For 0<q<10<q<10<q<1 the ordinary Bernoulli CLT gives

n(Fˉ^n(u)−q)⇒N(0,q(1−q)).\begin{gathered} \sqrt n\bigl(\widehat{\bar F}_n(u)-q\bigr)\\ \Rightarrow\mathcal N(0,q(1-q)). \end{gathered}n​(Fˉn​(u)−q)⇒N(0,q(1−q)).​
(11)

This is a pointwise CLT, not the functional Donsker theorem. The CDF value F(x)F(x)F(x) at a fixed xxx is a number; it is F(X)F(X)F(X) that is uniform for a continuous parent. At the random threshold MnM_nMn​, strict empirical survival is zero while the population survival has the Beta law above. The fixed-threshold binomial argument does not apply after replacing uuu by the observed maximum.

Small absolute probability error need not control an unbounded payoff. For instance, with δx\delta_xδx​ a unit mass at xxx, define

P0=δ0,Qε=(1−ε)δ0+εδε−2,0<ε<1.\begin{gathered} P_0=\delta_0,\\ Q_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)\delta_0 +\varepsilon\delta_{\varepsilon^{-2}},\\ 0<\varepsilon<1. \end{gathered}P0​=δ0​,Qε​=(1−ε)δ0​+εδε−2​,0<ε<1.​
(12)

Their CDFs differ by at most ε\varepsilonε, but their means differ by 1/ε1/\varepsilon1/ε. This proves that uniform CDF proximity alone does not bound mean error; an integrability or tail bound must supply the missing control. Inference asymmetry develops the broader finite-sample identification problem.

Rank plotting positions such as 1−i/(n+1)1-i/(n+1)1−i/(n+1) stay positive at the largest ordered observation and can differ at ties. They are not strict empirical survival. The existing tied-count example shows the distinction. Ordinary empirical bootstrap draws cannot exceed MnM_nMn​; a fitted-parent simulation can, conditional on its model assumptions. See max/sum calibration for the resampling limits. None of these facts makes empirical CDFs invalid.

Reproduction and source qualifications¶

Run uv run python demos/incerto/extreme_values.py for the two hidden-tail figures and the linked Gaussian/Hill examples. The formulas here are proved for exact Pareto or normal parents, not for every regularly varying tail at an unspecified finite cutoff. No empirical stock-loss multiplier is inferred.

SCoFT v4 §9.2, pp. 178–179, motivates hidden moments. Our domain is p<αp<\alphap<α; the complementary finite integral below a cutoff must not be identified with the divergent upper-tail integral when p≥αp\ge\alphap≥α. Section 9.2.1, pp. 179–180, supplies the normal comparison context; §9.3, pp. 180–181, motivates the distinction between probability and payoff accuracy Taleb, 2025. The pointwise CLT used above is the ordinary iid finite-variance CLT, as in Durrett (2019), Theorem 3.4.1, p. 144, in the January 2019 manuscript.
