Iso-Density Tail Geometry

Independent coordinates¶

Iso-density tail geometry studies the level sets of the iid joint density

fX1,X2(x1,x2)=f(x1)f(x2)f_{X_1,X_2}(x_1,x_2)=f(x_1)f(x_2)fX1​,X2​​(x1​,x2​)=f(x1​)f(x2​)
(1)

near a large-deviation constraint such as x1+x2=sx_1+x_2=sx1​+x2​=s. The diagnostic asks whether the most likely configurations along the constraint sit near the diagonal, where both coordinates are moderately large, or near the axes, where one coordinate is large and the other is ordinary.

Here X1,X2X_1,X_2X1​,X2​ are independent copies of a continuous random variable with density fff, and x1,x2x_1,x_2x1​,x2​ are its coordinate values. The sum level sss is fixed for each comparison. See Shared notation.

The same Cauchy marginals, a different joint law¶

The preceding calculation proves a comparison under independence. A spherical bivariate Cauchy has instead the normalized density

g(x1,x2)=12π(1+x12+x22)−3/2.g(x_1,x_2)=\frac{1}{2\pi}(1+x_1^2+x_2^2)^{-3/2}.g(x1​,x2​)=2π1​(1+x12​+x22​)−3/2.
(9)

The multivariate Student construction derives its normalization and Cauchy marginals at ν=1\nu=1ν=1. Its density is strictly decreasing in squared radius. The same completed-square identity used for the Gaussian therefore proves that, on x1+x2=sx_1+x_2=sx1​+x2​=s, its density is maximized at the equal split for every sss. Heavy marginal tails alone do not determine the constrained density geometry.

Switching correlation can destroy ellipticality¶

Consider an equal mixture of two centered bivariate normals with unit marginal variances and correlations rrr and −r-r−r, where 0<r<10<r<10<r<1. Its covariance is I2I_2I2​, and conditioning gives characteristic function

φ(t1,t2)=e−(t12+t22)/2cosh⁡(rt1t2).\varphi(t_1,t_2)=e^{-(t_1^2+t_2^2)/2}\cosh(rt_1t_2).φ(t1​,t2​)=e−(t12​+t22​)/2cosh(rt1​t2​).
(11)

We prove that this mixture is not elliptical. For a nondegenerate elliptical law with finite covariance, covariance is a positive scalar multiple of scatter: whitening gives a rotationally invariant law, whose covariance commutes with every orthogonal matrix and hence is scalar. Thus an elliptical law with covariance I2I_2I2​ must be spherical. But the displayed characteristic function at (v,0)(v,0)(v,0) is e−v2/2e^{-v^2/2}e−v2/2, whereas at (v/2,v/2)(v/\sqrt2,v/\sqrt2)(v/2​,v/2​) it is e−v2/2cosh⁡(rv2/2)e^{-v^2/2}\cosh(rv^2/2)e−v2/2cosh(rv2/2). For v≠0v\ne0v=0 these differ at the same radius. This disproves sphericality and hence ellipticality. Both marginal laws remain standard normal.

The iid Cauchy vector gives another distinction: every linear combination is Cauchy, with scale ∣a∣+∣b∣|a|+|b|∣a∣+∣b∣ for coefficients a,ba,ba,b not both zero, since its characteristic function is e−∣t1∣−∣t2∣e^{-|t_1|-|t_2|}e−∣t1​∣−∣t2​∣. It is a stable vector but not elliptical. To see the latter without moments, its characteristic function on either axis would force the elliptical profile to be exponential in the square root of its argument. It is also unchanged when one coordinate changes sign, forcing off-diagonal scatter zero. The equal marginal scales would then force circular level sets; ∣t1∣+∣t2∣|t_1|+|t_2|∣t1​∣+∣t2​∣ instead has diamond level sets. Thus closure under linear combinations does not require ellipticality.

Use bivariate_student.

import numpy as np
from xmath.incerto.multivariate import bivariate_student

rng = np.random.default_rng(20261020)
sample = bivariate_student(rng, nu=1, rho=0, size=10000)
radius = np.linalg.norm(sample, axis=1)
angle = np.arctan2(sample[:, 1], sample[:, 0])
sector = (radius > 3) & (angle >= 0) & (angle <= np.pi / 3)
print(f"Sector frequency: {sector.mean():.4g}")
print(f"Exact probability: {1 / (6 * np.sqrt(10)):.4g}")

Caveats¶

  • Iso-density geometry requires a density. Discrete, singular, censored, or heavily rounded data need a different diagnostic.

  • Density at a point is not probability mass. The contour picture explains local geometry; probability statements require integration over regions.

  • The iid product-density formula fails under dependence. Volatility clustering, common factors, and contagion can rotate or bend the contours.

  • The examples here are symmetric and centered. Skewed losses or one-sided positive variables should first be put into a problem-specific coordinate system.
