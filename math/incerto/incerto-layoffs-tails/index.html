Tech layoffs — counts and workforce losses

A cut of 100 people and a cut of 50% of a workforce measure different things. The first measures the number of jobs lost; the second measures the fraction removed from a company. A large employer can dominate the first distribution without making an unusually large proportional cut.

For people laid off per event, the empirical reading is fat tail, tail index about 1. The Hill estimator gives 1.04 using the largest 10% of observed counts and 1.01 using the largest 20%. A fitted pure power law nevertheless fails the goodness-of-fit check below. “Fat tail” here describes the observed upper range; these diagnostics do not establish an asymptotic tail class.

Data and the observed unit¶

The source is layoffs.fyi through the Kaggle mirror swaptr/layoffs-2022, retrieved 2026-10-26 layoffs.fyi & swaptr, 2026. Events run from 2020-03-11 to 2026-10-22. We pool countries, industries, and years in that snapshot.

We trim company names and group the 4,644 source rows by company and date. For same-day rows we sum available counts and retain the largest reported workforce fraction. An all-missing measurement stays missing. This gives 4,621 company-date events, of which 3,014 have counts. Dates on different days remain separate even for the same company. Exact repeated rows are included in the same aggregation; this is a reproducible reporting rule, not verification that every source row describes distinct jobs.

For percentages, 2,891 events have a reported fraction. We separate 377 reported 100% cuts, exclude one zero from a positive-tail calculation, and leave 1,730 missing fractions unfilled. The second sample therefore contains 2,513 events with a reported fraction strictly between 0 and 1. It is not restricted to events with an observed count. No inferred headcounts or filled-in percentages enter either figure.

A company-date event is neither a unique firm nor an independently sampled worker. Repeated employers, common economic conditions, and selective reporting all affect inference from this pooled sample.

Reading a Hill plot¶

Let x1:n≤⋯≤xn:nx_{1:n}\leq\cdots\leq x_{n:n}x1:n​≤⋯≤xn:n​ be the positive observations in ascending order. For 1≤k<n1\le k<n1≤k<n, we use the kkk largest values and the next value, xn−k:nx_{n-k:n}xn−k:n​, as the threshold. The Hill estimate and its reciprocal are

ξ^k,n=1k∑j=1klog⁡xn−j+1:nxn−k:n,\widehat\xi_{k,n}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k} \log\frac{x_{n-j+1:n}}{x_{n-k:n}},ξ​k,n​=k1​j=1∑k​logxn−k:n​xn−j+1:n​​,
(1)
α^k,n=1/ξ^k,n.\widehat\alpha_{k,n}=1/\widehat\xi_{k,n}.αk,n​=1/ξ​k,n​.
(2)

Here α\alphaα is the survival-tail exponent, following the shared notation; it is one less than the density exponent used in some power-law software. The relation ξ=1/α\xi=1/\alphaξ=1/α belongs to the positive regularly varying tail setting. Computing the statistic does not verify that setting Hill, 1975.

Increasing kkk brings more observations into the calculation and lowers the threshold. Very small kkk uses little information; large kkk can include the body of the distribution. Threshold selection explains this bias–variability tradeoff. Ties contribute zero log spacings; we keep them rather than jittering the observations. Thus the strict exceedance count can be smaller than kkk; if all selected values equal the threshold, ξ^k,n=0\widehat\xi_{k,n}=0ξ​k,n​=0 and there is no finite reciprocal estimate.

Both plots show kkk from 25 to roughly one third of the available sample. The shaded 95% pointwise bootstrap intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1,000 full-sample resamples, holding kkk fixed. They describe resampling variability under an iid approximation. They are not a simultaneous band, do not account for employer clustering or missingness, and do not confer guaranteed confidence coverage on an extreme-order statistic. The markers identify the largest 10% and 20%, rounded to integer kkk.

People laid off: an index near one¶

The two marked estimates are close: 1.04 at k=301k=301k=301 and 1.01 at k=603k=603k=603. Their pointwise bootstrap intervals are [0.94, 1.21] and [0.96, 1.13]. The curve lets us see the intervening variation instead of presenting a single selected threshold as settled. Round-number counts make steps visible as the threshold crosses tied values.

An index near one is a useful description of the severity of the observed count tail. It also warns against treating an ordinary average as a stable summary of exposure to large cuts. It does not establish an infinite population mean. The Pareto moment boundary is a statement about an exact unbounded model; actual layoffs are constrained by workforces, and the pure model is rejected here.

Percentages: use the loss of the retained workforce¶

Let ppp denote the reported fraction of the workforce laid off. Define the local log-loss variable

ℓ=−log⁡(1−p),0≤p<1.\ell=-\log(1-p),\qquad 0\leq p<1.ℓ=−log(1−p),0≤p<1.
(3)

Here ℓ\ellℓ is workforce log loss, with the same positive-loss sign convention as the S&P loss variable. It is dimensionless, and all logarithms are natural. The inverse maps a log loss back to the reported fraction:

p=1−e−ℓ.p=1-e^{-\ell}.p=1−e−ℓ.
(4)

This is the lower-bound-zero, upper-bound-one case of Cirillo and Taleb’s bounded-to-unbounded transformation Cirillo & Taleb, 2015 (equation 1). The coordinate maps [0,1)[0,1)[0,1) onto [0,∞)[0,\infty)[0,∞): ℓ=0\ell=0ℓ=0 at no loss, and ℓ\ellℓ diverges as the retained fraction 1−p1-p1−p tends to zero. For example, 50%, 90%, and 99% cuts give log losses of 0.69, 2.30, and 4.61. Thus the transformation separates large proportional cuts that a bounded percentage scale compresses near 100%.

Removing the coordinate’s endpoint does not produce a fat tail by itself. For example, if a hypothetical fraction is uniform on (0,1)(0,1)(0,1), then for u≥0u\geq0u≥0,

P(ℓ>u)=P(p>1−e−u)=e−u.\begin{aligned} &P(\ell>u)\\ &\quad=P(p>1-e^{-u})\\ &\quad=e^{-u}. \end{aligned}​P(ℓ>u)=P(p>1−e−u)=e−u.​
(5)

That transformed variable is exponential. This calculation establishes only that the transformation does not determine a tail class; the layoffs figures remain empirical diagnostics.

Endpoint rule: a reported p=1p=1p=1 gives an infinite log loss. We count those 377 events separately and exclude them from the finite-log-loss fit. We do not replace 100% by 99% or by 1−ε1-\varepsilon1−ε. A reported full-workforce cut need not establish a legal company closure. A zero fraction maps to zero and is excluded because Hill uses positive observations. Missing percentages are excluded without imputation. The figure therefore describes reported partial cuts, conditional on 0<p<10<p<10<p<1; it says nothing about the probability of a full cut among all companies.

The marked estimates are 1.98 at k=251k=251k=251 and 2.28 at k=503k=503k=503, with pointwise intervals [1.73, 2.45] and [1.92, 2.50]. They are larger and more threshold-sensitive than the count estimates. The reported fractions are rounded, with many ties; a nonlinear transform preserves those ties. The largest included fraction is 98%, so the largest observed finite ℓ\ellℓ is only 3.91. These estimates describe that visible range. They do not establish a Pareto tail for ℓ\ellℓ, and they are not exponents for raw percentages. The “index about 1” conclusion applies to counts.

Does an exact power-law tail fit?¶

We add the Clauset–Shalizi–Newman fit Clauset et al., 2009 to distinguish an exponent estimate from a model check. For each distinct candidate threshold, fit a continuous Pareto tail to all values at or above it; retain candidates with at least 50 observations and choose the smallest two-sided empirical KS distance. Threshold ties are included. Write uuu for the selected cutoff (the code calls it xmin).

SampleuuuTail eventsα^\widehat\alphaαKS
Counts230 people6811.040.053
Reported log loss0.3713871.890.114

The second threshold corresponds to a 31% workforce cut. The conditional fixed-threshold standard errors are 0.04 and 0.10 respectively. They omit threshold selection and model error; they are not the Hill intervals. The CSN inclusive tail count also differs from Hill’s fixed kkk when values at the cutoff tie.

The goodness-of-fit bootstrap resamples the empirical body and generates the fitted continuous tail, then refits both cutoff and exponent in each replicate. For each sample, zero of 1,000 replicates had KS distance at least as large as the observed distance. The recorded estimate is p^GOF=0\widehat p_{\mathrm{GOF}}=0p​GOF​=0; this is a finite Monte Carlo count, not a claim of zero probability. Both fits are rejected by the usual 0.1 CSN screening rule.

This test uses continuous tails for rounded observations. Such a null does not reproduce the piles at common counts and percentages, which can increase the observed KS distance relative to simulated samples. A discrete or explicit rounding model would answer a different question. Dependence is another reason to treat the nominal calibration cautiously. The present result rejects the specified pure continuous power-law description; it does not reject every fat-tailed alternative.

We stop at this comparison. Additional distributions can fit similar finite ranges, and a likelihood ranking would not repair a failed absolute fit. Company-size estimates would introduce a separate headcount and imputation problem. Neither is needed for the two observables examined here.

Reproduce the calculation¶

No layoffs rows are distributed with this page. Figures and aggregate outputs are generated beforehand; the site build neither downloads nor reads the local CSV. Install the Kaggle CLI separately, configure your own credentials, and run:

UV_NO_SYNC=1 uv run python scripts/download_layoffs.py
UV_NO_SYNC=1 uv run python demos/incerto/layoffs_tails.py --data data/incerto/cache/layoffs/layoffs.csv

Downloads remain under the ignored cache directory. A later Kaggle download may have changed; the numbers here use the dated snapshot above. The loader accepts both the raw mirror columns and the research-clean n/frac columns. load_layoffs calls clean_layoffs to apply the company-date rule. Set INCERTO_LAYOFFS_PATH to an existing CSV for the example below; without that variable, the loader uses the default cache.

Use hill_alpha_estimator, select_xmin, and workforce_log_loss.

Code: counts, log loss, and selected thresholds
import os
from xmath.incerto.datasets import load_layoffs, workforce_log_loss
from xmath.incerto.estimators import hill_alpha_estimator
from xmath.incerto.powerlaw import select_xmin

data = load_layoffs(os.environ.get("INCERTO_LAYOFFS_PATH"))
p = data.fraction
samples = {"Counts": data.count[data.count > 0],
           "Log loss": workforce_log_loss(p[(p > 0) & (p < 1)])}
print(f"Events: {data.count.size}")
for name, x in samples.items():
    print(f"{name}: {len(x)} observations")
    for fraction in (0.1, 0.2):
        k = round(fraction * len(x))
        print(f"  Hill k={k}: {hill_alpha_estimator(x, k):.2f}")
    fit = select_xmin(x, min_tail=50)
    print(f"  CSN cutoff={fit['xmin']:.3f}, tail={fit['n_tail']}, alpha={fit['alpha']:.2f}")

The figure script uses hill_stability_bootstrap for the bands, powerlaw_gof_bootstrap for the refitted model checks, and powerlaw_standard_error for the conditional errors. It draws through the shared set_theme, vertical_figure, and savefig helpers.

The seeds are 20261029 for counts and 20261030 for reported log loss, with the generator reset for each method. Point estimates match the reference refit. Interval endpoints can differ slightly because that refit consumes one random stream across five analyses; this page shares a resample across all kkk within each plotted curve. No infinite bootstrap estimates occurred on the plotted grid.

The recorded aggregate output and summary JSON retain sample sizes, exclusions, the four reported Hill estimates and intervals, and the CSN and GOF summaries. Full Hill curves and order statistics are not stored in the JSON. To print the saved summary without the CSV:

UV_NO_SYNC=1 uv run python demos/incerto/layoffs_tails.py --recorded assets/incerto/outputs/layoffs-tails.json

Regenerating the SVGs requires the local CSV and the --data command above. The demo keeps the plotting curves in memory and writes only the summaries.
