Max-to-Sum Ratio

Statement¶

Extreme-dominance intuition¶

In a thin-tailed or comfortable finite-mean sample, the largest observation may be memorable, but it should eventually become a negligible fraction of the whole. The total grows like nμn\munμ, while the largest draw grows more slowly than nnn on the scale needed to dominate the sum.

Fat-tailed samples can look different. One observation can represent a large fraction of all observed mass, especially near or below the Pareto mean boundary α=1\alpha=1α=1. This is the same mechanism behind LLN Failure Under Infinite Mean, but RnR_nRn​ makes the dominance visible without plotting the entire running average. It complements the path view in Pre-Asymptotic LLN Behavior.

Finite-mean proof¶

We prove the finite-mean theorem: if the iid nonnegative observations have a positive finite mean, then the maximum becomes a negligible share of the sum. The proof uses the strong law and Borel--Cantelli. The infinite-mean Pareto regimes are cited or described heuristically, because their exact limits need heavier regular-variation machinery.

Assume Xi≥0X_i\ge0Xi​≥0 are iid and 0<μ=E[X]<∞0<\mu=\mathbb E[X]<\infty0<μ=E[X]<∞. The strong law gives Sn/n→μS_n/n\to\muSn​/n→μ almost surely. It remains to show that Mn/n→0M_n/n\to0Mn​/n→0 almost surely.

For any ε>0\varepsilon>0ε>0, integrability implies

∑n=1∞P(Xn>εn)<∞,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb P(X_n>\varepsilon n)<\infty,n=1∑∞​P(Xn​>εn)<∞,
(3)

because the sum is bounded by a constant multiple of ∫0∞P(X>t) dt=E[X]\int_0^\infty\mathbb P(X>t)\,dt=\mathbb E[X]∫0∞​P(X>t)dt=E[X]. By Borel--Cantelli, Xn/n→0X_n/n\to0Xn​/n→0 almost surely. Therefore Mn/n→0M_n/n\to0Mn​/n→0 almost surely: after a random finite index all new observations are at most εn\varepsilon nεn, while the finitely many old observations divided by nnn vanish. Hence

MnSn=Mn/nSn/n→0\frac{M_n}{S_n}=\frac{M_n/n}{S_n/n}\to0Sn​Mn​​=Sn​/nMn​/n​→0

almost surely.

For a Pareto Type I tail, Pareto Moment Existence shows that the mean exists exactly when α>1\alpha>1α>1. The regimes are qualitatively different:

  • α>1\alpha>1α>1: Mn/Sn→0M_n/S_n\to0Mn​/Sn​→0 almost surely by the finite-mean theorem.

  • Exact Pareto α=1\alpha=1α=1: Mn/Sn→0M_n/S_n\to0Mn​/Sn​→0 in probability, but slowly; Mn/(xmn)M_n/(x_m n)Mn​/(xm​n) is bounded in probability, while Sn/(xmnlog⁡n)→1S_n/(x_m n\log n)\to1Sn​/(xm​nlogn)→1 in probability (a cited limit). This is not almost-sure convergence of the ratio.

  • 0<α<10<\alpha<10<α<1: under standard regular-variation assumptions, Mn/SnM_n/S_nMn​/Sn​ has a nondegenerate limiting distribution Darling, 1952. Infinite mean does not imply that one observation asymptotically equals the whole sum; several of the largest observations may keep material shares.

For iid nonnegative variables with P(X>0)>0P(X>0)>0P(X>0)>0, the converse to the finite-mean theorem also holds: Rn→0R_n\to0Rn​→0 almost surely if and only if E[X]<∞\mathbb E[X]<\inftyE[X]<∞. This converse is cited from O'Brien (1980), not proved above. It does not rule out convergence in probability with an infinite mean, as the exact Pareto α=1\alpha=1α=1 case shows.

For an exact Pareto law with α>1\alpha>1α>1, the Frechet maximum scale is xmn1/αx_m n^{1/\alpha}xm​n1/α, whereas the sum is asymptotic to nμn\munμ. Thus a typical ratio has scale n1/α−1n^{1/\alpha-1}n1/α−1: its exponent is −3/13-3/13−3/13 at α=1.3\alpha=1.3α=1.3 and −2/3-2/3−2/3 at α=3\alpha=3α=3. This scale comparison supplies no universal finite-nnn quantile or confidence bound. The infinite-mean limits cited above require separate asymptotic results; the simulation below illustrates finite samples.

print("Zero prefix: [" + ", ".join(f"{v:.4g}" for v in max_to_sum_path([0, 2, 2])) + "]")

Reference distributions for the diagnostic¶

Use model-based simulation as the primary reference: specify or fit an iid parent distribution on nonnegative values, simulate independent samples of the observed size nnn, and compute RnR_nRn​ for each sample. Compare the observed rnr_nrn​ with that simulated distribution. Report the parent model, fitted parameters (including α\alphaα for a Pareto model), sample size, simulation count, and seed. The reference assumes the stated parent law is appropriate; a fitted-model comparison is conditional on the fit and needs a separate assessment of parameter uncertainty before it is used as a calibrated test. A tail-only fit also needs a model for the body when SnS_nSn​ includes all observations. A tail exponent alone does not determine a finite-nnn reference. If the model permits zero totals, state whether the reference is conditional on Sn>0S_n>0Sn​>0.

As a complement, use mmm-out-of-nnn subsampling without replacement, with m→∞m\to\inftym→∞ and m/n→0m/n\to0m/n→0: compute the ratio on all size-mmm subsets, or approximate that distribution using many uniformly sampled subsets. Validity requires a limiting law for the statistic with the normalization appropriate to the parent tail. For example, for a stated exact Pareto exponent, the preceding maximum and sum limits give the following local normalizing factors ckc_kck​ for ckRkc_kR_kck​Rk​:

The Pareto note proves the boundary normalizations and cites the nondegenerate infinite-mean limit. Compare cnrnc_nr_ncn​rn​ with the subsample values cmrmc_mr_mcm​rm​, using the same model parameters. Raw mmm-sample quantiles are not nnn-sample quantiles when cm≠cnc_m\ne c_ncm​=cn​. For a general regularly varying parent, slowly varying factors can change the normalization. If its parameters are estimated, justify that their substitution leaves the normalized limit unchanged; mere consistency of an exponent estimate need not control a power of nnn.

For iid data, a nondegenerate weak limit of the normalized statistic and m/n→0m/n\to0m/n→0 justify convergence of the subsampling distribution at continuity points of the limiting CDF. Estimated centering or normalization must have negligible effect at the subsample scale. We cite this result from Politis & Romano (1994); Bickel, Götze, and van Zwet (1997), Theorem 1, p. 9 gives a proof and an explicit plug-in condition. These are asymptotic conditions; report the chosen mmm and sensitivity to it.

Ordinary nnn-out-of-nnn empirical bootstrap validity is not assumed here. Its maximum cannot exceed the observed maximum, and with a unique sample maximum it equals that maximum with conditional probability 1−(1−1/n)n→1−e−11-(1-1/n)^n\to1-e^{-1}1−(1−1/n)n→1−e−1. This can obstruct a continuous extreme-value limit. Heavy-tailed sums can also have non-Gaussian stable limits for which ordinary bootstrap approximations fail; see Bickel, Götze, and van Zwet (1997), §2.3, Example 4, p. 5. The finite-mean theorem for RnR_nRn​ proves no resampling validity statement.

Caveats¶

  • RnR_nRn​ is a dominance diagnostic, not an estimator of the tail exponent.

  • A small RnR_nRn​ does not prove thin tails. A sample may simply not have seen a record-sized observation yet.

  • A large RnR_nRn​ can also come from data errors, censoring, mixtures, dependence, or nonstationarity. Inspect the observation before interpreting the ratio as a tail fact.

  • RnR_nRn​ has no universal “large” threshold. Compare an observed value with a reference distribution under the model and calibration assumptions above.

  • For signed returns, Mn/SnM_n/S_nMn​/Sn​ may be unstable or meaningless because of cancellation. Apply it to nonnegative losses, severities, exposures, or possibly ∣Xi∣|X_i|∣Xi​∣, explicitly changing the interpretation.

  • A running max-to-sum curve is order-dependent, while the final RnR_nRn​ is not.

  • For 0<α<10<\alpha<10<α<1, exact limiting behavior of Mn/SnM_n/S_nMn​/Sn​ belongs to stable and Poisson point process asymptotics. Here those limits are cited from Darling (1952) and Resnick (2007); only the finite-mean implication is proved.
