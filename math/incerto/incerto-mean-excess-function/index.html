Mean Excess Function

Statement¶

For a random variable XXX and a threshold uuu with P(X>u)>0\mathbb P(X>u)>0P(X>u)>0 and finite conditional expectation above uuu, the mean excess function is

e(u)=E[X−u∣X>u].e(u)=\mathbb E[X-u\mid X>u].e(u)=E[X−u∣X>u].
(1)

It measures the expected overshoot beyond uuu after the threshold has already been crossed. For a Pareto Type I tail with exponent α>1\alpha>1α>1 and u≥xmu\ge x_mu≥xm​,

e(u)=uα−1.e(u)=\frac{u}{\alpha-1}.e(u)=α−1u​.
(2)

For a generalized Pareto variable with location zero, shape ξ\xiξ, scale β>0\beta>0β>0, and threshold uuu in its support with β+ξu>0\beta+\xi u>0β+ξu>0, the mean excess function is linear when ξ<1\xi<1ξ<1:

e(u)=β+ξu1−ξ.e(u)=\frac{\beta+\xi u}{1-\xi}.e(u)=1−ξβ+ξu​.
(3)

The exponential distribution is the boundary case ξ=0\xi=0ξ=0, where e(u)e(u)e(u) is constant. Here uuu is the threshold, ξ\xiξ the GPD shape, and β\betaβ its scale at threshold zero.

Diagnostic intuition¶

The mean excess function asks what remains after an event is already large. Thin-tail intuition often expects the remaining excess to be tame once a high threshold has been crossed. A Pareto tail says the opposite: the expected additional excess grows in proportion to the threshold itself.

The exponential, positive-shape GPD with ξ<1\xi<1ξ<1, and negative-shape GPD models have constant, increasing linear, and decreasing linear population mean excess functions, respectively. Curvature departs from an exact GPD model over that threshold range. An empirical plot can also bend because of sampling noise, especially when few observations exceed the threshold; its shape alone does not identify a model failure or a body-to-tail transition.

Empirical values are diagnostics; high thresholds aim to reduce tail-model bias but leave fewer exceedances and more noise.

Population curves and sample estimates¶

Use mean_excess; masking retains the counts and reports an unavailable estimate as nan:

from xmath.incerto.estimators import mean_excess

result = mean_excess([1, 2, 2, 4], [1, 2, 4], min_exceedances=2)
for u, count, mean in zip(result["threshold"], result["exceedances"], result["mean_excess"]):
    print(f"u={u:.4g}: count={int(count):d}, mean_excess={mean:.4g}")

Output

u=1: count=3, mean_excess=1.667
u=2: count=1, mean_excess=nan
u=4: count=0, mean_excess=nan

Regenerate the figure with uv run python demos/incerto/tail_estimation.py. The threshold comparison adds the shared mixture sample, whose body changes the population curve below 3.

Caveats¶

  • The mean excess function is itself a mean. If the fitted tail has ξ≥1\xi\ge1ξ≥1, the theoretical mean excess is infinite.

  • Empirical mean excess plots are unstable at high thresholds. Always inspect exceedance counts.

  • Linear-looking behavior is suggestive, not decisive. Mixtures and finite upper truncation can create misleading curvature.

  • For two-sided returns, apply the function to a one-sided loss variable or to absolute returns after stating the modeling choice.
