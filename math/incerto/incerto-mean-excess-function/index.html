Mean Excess Function

Statement¶

For a real random variable XXX with CDF FFF and a finite threshold uuu with Fˉ(u)=P(X>u)>0\bar F(u)=\mathbb P(X>u)>0Fˉ(u)=P(X>u)>0, the mean excess function is

(1)

Here (X−u)+=max⁡(X−u,0)(X-u)_+=\max(X-u,0)(X−u)+​=max(X−u,0). The conditional excess is nonnegative, so this definition allows e(u)=+∞e(u)=+\inftye(u)=+∞; it is finite exactly when that excess is integrable. It measures the expected overshoot beyond uuu after the threshold has already been crossed. For a Pareto Type I tail with exponent α>1\alpha>1α>1 and u≥xmu\ge x_mu≥xm​,

e(u)=uα−1.e(u)=\frac{u}{\alpha-1}.e(u)=α−1u​.
(2)

Diagnostic intuition¶

The mean excess function asks what remains after an event is already large. Thin-tail intuition often expects the remaining excess to be tame once a high threshold has been crossed. A Pareto tail says the opposite: the expected additional excess grows in proportion to the threshold itself.

The exponential, positive-shape GPD with ξ<1\xi<1ξ<1, and negative-shape GPD models have constant, increasing linear, and decreasing linear population mean excess functions, respectively. Curvature departs from an exact GPD model over that threshold range. An empirical plot can also bend because of sampling noise, especially when few observations exceed the threshold; its shape alone does not identify a model failure or a body-to-tail transition.

Pareto derivation and GPD sketch¶

We derive the Pareto mean-excess formula from the conditional survival function. The generalized Pareto linear formula follows from standard GPD threshold stability and the GPD mean formula. The GPD page derives the stability identity; its moment formulas are cited rather than reproved here.

For Pareto Type I,

Empirical values are diagnostics; high thresholds aim to reduce tail-model bias but leave fewer exceedances and more noise.

Population curves and sample estimates¶

Use mean_excess; masking retains the counts and reports an unavailable estimate as nan:

from xmath.incerto.estimators import mean_excess

result = mean_excess([1, 2, 2, 4], [1, 2, 4], min_exceedances=2)
for u, count, mean in zip(result["threshold"], result["exceedances"], result["mean_excess"]):
    print(f"u={u:.4g}: count={int(count):d}, mean_excess={mean:.4g}")

From overshoot to an unconditional tail contribution¶

For an integrable upper tail with Fˉ(u)>0\bar F(u)>0Fˉ(u)>0, conditioning proves

E[X1{X>u}]=Fˉ(u){u+e(u)}.\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_{\{X>u\}}] =\bar F(u)\{u+e(u)\}.E[X1{X>u}​]=Fˉ(u){u+e(u)}.
(10)

The factor Fˉ(u)\bar F(u)Fˉ(u) and the threshold term uuu are both needed. For an exact Pareto parent with α>1\alpha>1α>1, the formula agrees with the hidden mean above a sample maximum. For α≤1\alpha\le1α≤1, both the conditional mean excess and the hidden first moment are infinite at every finite admissible threshold, even though a nonempty finite exceedance sample yields a finite empirical mean excess.

At the observed maximum, the empirical excess has no observations and is undefined. The existing runnable count example prints nan there; it does not supply evidence for a bounded population. The finite-endpoint bound and nonmonotone counterexample earlier on this page still apply. SCoFT v4 §§9.2–9.3, pp. 177–181, motivates this distinction Taleb, 2025.

Caveats¶

  • The mean excess function is itself a mean. If the fitted tail has ξ≥1\xi\ge1ξ≥1, the theoretical mean excess is infinite.

  • Empirical mean excess plots are unstable at high thresholds. Always inspect exceedance counts.

  • Linear-looking behavior is suggestive, not decisive. Mixtures and finite upper truncation can create misleading curvature.

  • For two-sided returns, apply the function to a one-sided loss variable or to absolute returns after stating the modeling choice.
