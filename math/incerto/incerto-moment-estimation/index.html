Moment estimation and its limits

A finite sample produces a finite empirical moment in exact arithmetic. It does not establish that the corresponding population moment exists. Even when that moment exists, the estimator can have infinite variance or be dominated by events that most samples miss. We separate these questions using an exact Pareto model and a finite Gaussian mixture.

The sample notation follows the shared canon. For iid Xi≥0X_i\ge0Xi​≥0 and order q>0q>0q>0, write

mq=EXq,m^q,n=1n∑i=1nXiq.\begin{aligned} m_q&=\mathbb E X^q,\\ \widehat m_{q,n}&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i^q. \end{aligned}mq​mq,n​​=EXq,=n1​i=1∑n​Xiq​.​
(1)

These are raw moments. For a signed variable, replace XqX^qXq by ∣X∣q|X|^q∣X∣q; a signed odd moment requires separate integrability conditions.

The estimator needs a higher moment¶

For Pareto with α,xm>0\alpha,x_m>0α,xm​>0,

mq=αxmqα−q,q<α,mq=∞,q≥α.\begin{aligned} m_q&=\frac{\alpha x_m^q}{\alpha-q},\\ &\hspace{2em}q<\alpha,\\ m_q&=\infty,\\ &\hspace{2em}q\ge\alpha. \end{aligned}mq​mq​​=α−qαxmq​​,q<α,=∞,q≥α.​
(2)

Assume q<αq<\alphaq<α. Then the sample moment is unbiased for every n≥1n\ge1n≥1, but it has finite variance only if 2q<α2q<\alpha2q<α. In that regime,

nVar⁡(m^q,n)=αxm2qα−2q−(αxmqα−q)2.\begin{aligned} n\operatorname{Var}(\widehat m_{q,n}) &=\frac{\alpha x_m^{2q}}{\alpha-2q}\\ &\quad-\left(\frac{\alpha x_m^q}{\alpha-q}\right)^2. \end{aligned}nVar(mq,n​)​=α−2qαxm2q​​−(α−qαxmq​​)2.​
(3)

We prove both assertions. Linearity gives the expectation whenever mqm_qmq​ is finite. For 2q<α2q<\alpha2q<α, independence makes the cross-covariances zero. For q<α≤2qq<\alpha\le2qq<α≤2q, nonnegativity gives m^q,n2≥X12q/n2\widehat m_{q,n}^2\ge X_1^{2q}/n^2mq,n2​≥X12q​/n2, whose expectation is infinite. Since the estimator has finite mean, its variance is infinite too. Equality at 2q=α2q=\alpha2q=α is excluded by the logarithmic divergence of the Pareto moment integral.

For example, α=3,xm=1\alpha=3,x_m=1α=3,xm​=1 gives m2=3m_2=3m2​=3, but m^2,n\widehat m_{2,n}m2,n​ has infinite variance at every finite nnn. At α=6\alpha=6α=6, m2=3/2m_2=3/2m2​=3/2, m4=3m_4=3m4​=3, and the estimator variance is 3/(4n)3/(4n)3/(4n). A simulated finite standard error in the first model cannot certify the population variance of the estimator.

Consistency does not require estimator variance¶

We prove convergence in probability when mq<∞m_q<\inftymq​<∞, without a finite 2q2q2qth moment. For a fixed truncation level B>0B>0B>0, Chebyshev’s inequality gives convergence of the average of min⁡(Xiq,B)\min(X_i^q,B)min(Xiq​,B) to its expectation, because these variables are bounded and iid. The omitted nonnegative part has expected average E(X1q−B)+→0\mathbb E(X_1^q-B)_+\to0E(X1q​−B)+​→0 as B→∞B\to\inftyB→∞. Markov’s inequality controls that average uniformly in nnn. For any error tolerance, first choose BBB to make this expectation small, then nnn to control the bounded average. The triangle inequality proves m^q,n→mq\widehat m_{q,n}\to m_qmq,n​→mq​ in probability. This argument supplies no uniform rate over all integrable laws.

When mq=∞m_q=\inftymq​=∞, the stronger almost-sure divergence follows from the nonnegative infinite-mean theorem, applied to XiqX_i^qXiq​. That linked note proves the result by truncation. Finite observed values and floating-point overflow are separate matters; neither determines which population regime applies.

A square root changes the moment question¶

Let Rn=m^2,nR_n=\sqrt{\widehat m_{2,n}}Rn​=m2,n​​. If m2<∞m_2<\inftym2​<∞, then

ERn2=m2,ERn≤m2,Var⁡(Rn)<∞.\begin{gathered} \mathbb E R_n^2=m_2,\\ \mathbb E R_n\le\sqrt{m_2},\\ \operatorname{Var}(R_n)<\infty. \end{gathered}ERn2​=m2​,ERn​≤m2​​,Var(Rn​)<∞.​
(4)

The identity is immediate from the definition. Cauchy–Schwarz gives the inequality and integrability of RnR_nRn​; hence its variance is finite. Thus infinite variance of the empirical second moment does not imply infinite variance of the empirical root mean square. For the sample standard deviation sn2=n−1∑(Xi−Xˉn)2s_n^2=n^{-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X_n)^2sn2​=n−1∑(Xi​−Xˉn​)2, the identity sn2=m^2,n−Xˉn2≤m^2,ns_n^2=\widehat m_{2,n}-\bar X_n^2\le\widehat m_{2,n}sn2​=m2,n​−Xˉn2​≤m2,n​ gives the same finite-second-moment conclusion for sns_nsn​. A fixed n/(n−1)n/(n-1)n/(n−1) correction for n>1n>1n>1 does not change this conclusion.

The square root can introduce downward bias through concavity. The sample raw moment remains unbiased. Bias, estimator variance, and the frequency of an underestimate are different properties.

Here we know the center is zero and assume iid Xi∼N(0,σ2)X_i\sim N(0,\sigma^2)Xi​∼N(0,σ2), with σ>0\sigma>0σ>0. The last identity uses ERn2=σ2\mathbb ER_n^2=\sigma^2ERn2​=σ2. The corresponding MSE for AnA_nAn​, divided by σ2\sigma^2σ2, is (π/2−1)/n(\pi/2-1)/n(π/2−1)/n. This comparison does not carry over unchanged to the rare-state mixture: E∣X∣=2/π EY\mathbb E|X|=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\mathbb E\sqrt YE∣X∣=2/π​EY​, so the same AnA_nAn​ now targets EY\mathbb E\sqrt YEY​, whereas the population standard deviation is EY\sqrt{\mathbb EY}EY​. Finite moments that most samples miss¶

Use the two-state variance mixture with P(Y=0.5)=0.99P(Y=0.5)=0.99P(Y=0.5)=0.99, P(Y=50.5)=0.01P(Y=50.5)=0.01P(Y=50.5)=0.01, and X=YZX=\sqrt YZX=Y​Z, with independent standard-normal ZZZ. Draw the pairs (Yi,Zi)(Y_i,Z_i)(Yi​,Zi​) independently. The population second moment is 1 and fourth moment is 77.25, so

Var⁡(1n∑Xi2)=76.25n.\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac1n\sum X_i^2\right)=\frac{76.25}{n}.Var(n1​∑Xi2​)=n76.25​.
(6)

The probability that no wide component is selected is 0.99n0.99^n0.99n. Conditional on that event, all observations are independent N(0,0.5)N(0,0.5)N(0,0.5) and the empirical second moment has expectation 0.5. At n=50n=50n=50, this event has probability about 0.605. Rare samples compensate in the unconditional mean; the raw second moment estimator is still unbiased.

Rising power means and different targets¶

For fixed observations and positive weights wiw_iwi​ summing to 1, define Ap=(∑iwi∣xi∣p)1/pA_p=(\sum_iw_i|x_i|^p)^{1/p}Ap​=(∑i​wi​∣xi​∣p)1/p. For 0<p<q0<p<q0<p<q,

Mean absolute deviation is a different estimand¶

If μ=EX\mu=\mathbb EXμ=EX and E∣X∣<∞\mathbb E|X|<\inftyE∣X∣<∞, then E∣X−μ∣≤E∣X∣+∣μ∣<∞\mathbb E|X-\mu|\le\mathbb E|X|+|\mu|<\inftyE∣X−μ∣≤E∣X∣+∣μ∣<∞. If the variance is finite, Cauchy–Schwarz gives E∣X−μ∣≤Var⁡(X)\mathbb E|X-\mu|\le\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}E∣X−μ∣≤Var(X)​. Equality requires ∣X−μ∣|X-\mu|∣X−μ∣ to be constant almost surely. These are unscaled deviations; Gaussian calibration multiplies the mean absolute deviation by π/2\sqrt{\pi/2}π/2​, so that universal inequality does not apply to the calibrated value.

For example, the symmetric law on {−1,1}\{-1,1\}{−1,1} has standard deviation and unscaled mean absolute deviation both equal to 1. Its Gaussian-calibrated Std/MeanAD ratio is 2/π<1\sqrt{2/\pi}<12/π​<1. The Gaussian benchmark 1 in the dispersion diagnostic is a model reference, not a universal lower bound. Mean and median absolute deviations also answer different questions. Neither universally replaces a second-moment estimand or dominates every estimator under every loss.

Matching a Gaussian sample by absolute error¶

For iid integrable observations with mean μ\muμ, let M(n)=E∣Sn−nμ∣M(n)=\mathbb E|S_n-n\mu|M(n)=E∣Sn​−nμ∣ and assume M(1)>0M(1)>0M(1)>0. The kappa note compares this sum deviation at two sizes. Here the target is the relative mean absolute error of the sample mean:

R(n)=M(n)nM(1).R(n)=\frac{M(n)}{nM(1)}.R(n)=nM(1)M(n)​.
(9)

Scaling two parent laws to the same single-observation MAD makes R(n)R(n)R(n) a comparison in common units. For a Gaussian sample of size ngn_gng​, this relative error is ng−1/2n_g^{-1/2}ng−1/2​. Define the minimum matching integer by

n∗=min⁡{n∈N:n≥1, R(n)≤ng−1/2}.n_*=\min\{n\in\mathbb N:n\ge1,\ R(n)\le n_g^{-1/2}\}.n∗​=min{n∈N:n≥1, R(n)≤ng−1/2​}.
(10)

This criterion matches one expectation of an estimation error. It does not equate variances, quantiles, or confidence coverage. For example, Markov’s inequality gives only

P(∣Xˉn−μ∣>ε)≤M(n)nε,ε>0.\begin{gathered} P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\varepsilon) \le\frac{M(n)}{n\varepsilon},\\ \varepsilon>0. \end{gathered}P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣>ε)≤nεM(n)​,ε>0.​
(11)

The bound may be loose. There is no universal kappa cutoff that certifies a normal confidence interval. Under variance matching, every iid law with the same finite variance has the same variance of its sample mean at the same size; that is a different comparison.

Why the search is valid¶

We prove that R(n)R(n)R(n) is nonincreasing. Center n+1n+1n+1 observations and form the n+1n+1n+1 averages that each omit one observation. Their average is the full (n+1)(n+1)(n+1)-observation average. Convexity of absolute value and identical distributions therefore give

E∣Xˉn+1−μ∣≤E∣Xˉn−μ∣.\mathbb E|\bar X_{n+1}-\mu| \le\mathbb E|\bar X_n-\mu|.E∣Xˉn+1​−μ∣≤E∣Xˉn​−μ∣.
(12)

It also tends to zero under integrability. To see this, clip each centered variable to [−B,B][-B,B][−B,B] and center the clipped variable again. Its average has expected absolute value at most its standard deviation divided by n\sqrt nn​. The remaining centered part has expected absolute value at most twice the expected clipping error, uniformly in nnn, and this tends to zero as B→∞B\to\inftyB→∞. First take n→∞n\to\inftyn→∞, then B→∞B\to\inftyB→∞. Thus n∗n_*n∗​ exists for every finite integer ngn_gng​ under these assumptions. A finite computational search can still stop before reaching it.

When a constant kappa is enough¶

If a model has the exact power relation M(n)/M(1)=n1/(2−κ)M(n)/M(1)=n^{1/(2-\kappa)}M(n)/M(1)=n1/(2−κ) at every size, with constant κ<1\kappa<1κ<1, solving the matching inequality gives

n∗=⌈ng (2−κ)/[2(1−κ)]⌉.n_*=\left\lceil n_g^{\,(2-\kappa)/[2(1-\kappa)]}\right\rceil.n∗​=⌈ng(2−κ)/[2(1−κ)]​⌉.
(13)

For an integrable stable law, κ=2−α\kappa=2-\alphaκ=2−α and the exponent is α/[2(α−1)]\alpha/[2(\alpha-1)]α/[2(α−1)]. At α=1.5\alpha=1.5α=1.5 this exponent is 1.5. For a general law, substituting a small-size kappa into this formula is a heuristic. Its kappa changes with size, so the exact equation is implicit. The cubic Student and exponential examples use their full MAD curves.

Equal-weight exposures and apparent stabilization¶

For independent, identically distributed centered exposures Xi−μX_i-\muXi​−μ, the equally weighted portfolio fluctuation is n−1∑i(Xi−μ)n^{-1}\sum_i(X_i-\mu)n−1∑i​(Xi​−μ). Its MAD is exactly M(n)/nM(n)/nM(n)/n. The aggregation figure therefore also quantifies this narrowly specified diversification calculation. A smooth sample path or a visually concentrated center can still conceal substantial expected absolute error.

Dependence changes the result. If every exposure equals the same integrable random variable YYY, then the portfolio average is YYY at every size and its MAD never decreases. More generally, writing Xi=C+ϵiX_i=C+\epsilon_iXi​=C+ϵi​ with an integrable common component CCC independent of iid centered integrable ϵi\epsilon_iϵi​ gives an average converging to CCC in L1L^1L1. Its MAD about EC\mathbb ECEC consequently tends to E∣C−EC∣\mathbb E|C-\mathbb EC|E∣C−EC∣: apply the reverse triangle inequality and the L1L^1L1 averaging argument above. Increasing the number of components cannot remove the common fluctuation.

These model identities are the scope used for Taleb (2025), §§8.3.2/8.5, pp. 159–164. They do not establish an optimal allocation, a universal risk multiple, or empirical kappa values for stocks. For different weights and dependence, return to the multivariate note and shared-shock example.

Reproduce the moment-estimation experiment¶

Use pareto_type1, pareto_moment_estimator_summary.

Code
import numpy as np
from xmath.incerto.distributions import pareto_type1
from xmath.incerto.finite_moments import pareto_moment_estimator_summary

rng = np.random.default_rng(20261020)
for alpha in (3, 6):
    sample = pareto_type1.rvs(alpha, size=(100, 200), random_state=rng)
    estimates = np.mean(sample**2, axis=1)
    print(f"alpha={alpha:.4g}")
    for name, value in pareto_moment_estimator_summary(alpha, q=2, n=200).items():
        print(f"  {name}: {value:.4g}")
    quantiles = np.quantile(estimates, [0.1, 0.5, 0.9])
    print("Sample quantiles: [" + ", ".join(f"{v:.4g}" for v in quantiles) + "]")
