Pareto Moment Existence

Statement¶

Let XXX have a Pareto Type I distribution with lower cutoff xm>0x_m>0xm​>0 and tail exponent α>0\alpha>0α>0:

Fˉ(x)=P(X>x)=(xmx)α,x≥xm.\bar F(x)=\mathbb P(X>x)=\left(\frac{x_m}{x}\right)^\alpha, \qquad x\ge x_m.Fˉ(x)=P(X>x)=(xxm​​)α,x≥xm​.
(1)

For any moment order p>0p>0p>0,

E[Xp]<∞⟺p<α.\mathbb E[X^p] < \infty \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p<\alpha.E[Xp]<∞⟺p<α.
(2)

When p<αp<\alphap<α,

E[Xp]=αxmpα−p.\mathbb E[X^p]=\frac{\alpha x_m^p}{\alpha-p}.E[Xp]=α−pαxmp​​.
(3)

At p=αp=\alphap=α, the truncated moment diverges logarithmically. For p>αp>\alphap>α, it diverges as a power of the upper cutoff. In particular, the Pareto mean exists only for α>1\alpha>1α>1, and the variance exists only for α>2\alpha>2α>2.

Here Fˉ\bar FFˉ denotes survival probability and E\mathbb EE denotes expectation; ppp is a positive moment order.

Proof by direct integration¶

We prove the Pareto Type I moment boundary and the moment formula by direct integration. The same calculation also explains the logarithmic boundary and power-divergent regimes in the truncated-moment section. The broader regularly varying moment test is handled by Karamata’s Theorem.

For x≥xmx\ge x_mx≥xm​, the Pareto density is

f(x)=αxmαx−(α+1).f(x)=\alpha x_m^\alpha x^{-(\alpha+1)}.f(x)=αxmα​x−(α+1).
(4)

For p>0p>0p>0,

E[Xp]=∫xm∞xpαxmαx−(α+1) dx=αxmα∫xm∞xp−α−1 dx.\mathbb E[X^p] = \int_{x_m}^{\infty}x^p\alpha x_m^\alpha x^{-(\alpha+1)}\,dx = \alpha x_m^\alpha \int_{x_m}^{\infty}x^{p-\alpha-1}\,dx.E[Xp]=∫xm​∞​xpαxmα​x−(α+1)dx=αxmα​∫xm​∞​xp−α−1dx.
(5)

The integral converges exactly when p−α−1<−1p-\alpha-1<-1p−α−1<−1, equivalently p<αp<\alphap<α. Evaluating the convergent case gives

αxmα⋅xmp−αα−p=αxmpα−p.\alpha x_m^\alpha \cdot\frac{x_m^{p-\alpha}}{\alpha-p} = \frac{\alpha x_m^p}{\alpha-p}.αxmα​⋅α−pxmp−α​​=α−pαxmp​​.
(6)

Adding α+1\alpha+1α+1 to both sides proves the algebraic equivalence p−α−1<−1⟺p<αp-\alpha-1<-1 \Longleftrightarrow p<\alphap−α−1<−1⟺p<α.

This exact Pareto calculation is the simplest instance of the Karamata moment test for regularly varying tails.

Truncated-moment behavior¶

For a finite upper cutoff b≥xmb\ge x_mb≥xm​, define

Mp(b)=E[Xp1{X≤b}].M_p(b)=\mathbb E[X^p\mathbf 1_{\{X\le b\}}].Mp​(b)=E[Xp1{X≤b}​].
(7)

Direct integration gives

Mp(b)={αxmαα−p(xmp−α−bp−α),p<α,αxmαlog⁡(b/xm),p=α,αxmαp−α(bp−α−xmp−α),p>α.M_p(b)= \begin{cases} \dfrac{\alpha x_m^\alpha}{\alpha-p} \left(x_m^{p-\alpha}-b^{p-\alpha}\right), & p<\alpha,\\[1.1em] \alpha x_m^\alpha\log(b/x_m), & p=\alpha,\\[0.8em] \dfrac{\alpha x_m^\alpha}{p-\alpha} \left(b^{p-\alpha}-x_m^{p-\alpha}\right), & p>\alpha. \end{cases}Mp​(b)=⎩⎨⎧​α−pαxmα​​(xmp−α​−bp−α),αxmα​log(b/xm​),p−ααxmα​​(bp−α−xmp−α​),​p<α,p=α,p>α.​
(8)

Thus the same theorem has three operational regimes: convergence to a finite moment, logarithmic boundary growth, and power growth.

Products and moment integrals¶

Use pareto_truncated_moment.

from xmath.incerto.power_laws import pareto_truncated_moment

for order in (1, 1.5, 2):
    moments = pareto_truncated_moment([10, 100, 10000], alpha=1.5, order=order)
    print(f"order={order:.4g}: [" + ", ".join(f"{v:.4g}" for v in moments) + "]")

Examples¶

  • If α=0.8\alpha=0.8α=0.8, the mean is infinite because 1≥α1\ge\alpha1≥α.

  • If α=1.5\alpha=1.5α=1.5, the mean exists, but the variance is infinite because 2≥α2\ge\alpha2≥α.

  • If α=3\alpha=3α=3, the mean and variance exist, while the third raw moment is at the logarithmic boundary and diverges.

The theorem is exact, but sample estimates can still look misleading. With α\alphaα close to a boundary, a finite run can appear calm until a new large observation changes the empirical moment.

For α=1.2\alpha=1.2α=1.2 and xm=1x_m=1xm​=1, the population mean is 6 while the second raw moment is infinite. Any finite sample of finite observations still has a finite sample second moment. Its finiteness does not settle the population question.

Caveats¶

  • Moment existence is a property of the generating distribution, not proof that a finite sample estimate will be accurate.

  • When α\alphaα is close to a boundary, convergence can be so slow that the formal moment is a poor operational summary.

  • For two-sided heavy-tailed variables, check absolute moments or one-sided tails explicitly. Symmetry can make a location parameter look finite while absolute exposure is infinite.

  • Empirical Pareto fits require threshold checks. A moment calculation using a fitted α^\widehat\alphaα inherits the uncertainty and bias of the tail fit.
