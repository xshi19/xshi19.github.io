Pareto Distribution

Overview¶

How quickly does the chance of a large observation fall as we raise the threshold? The Pareto law models a positive quantity above a lower cutoff. Its tail exponent α\alphaα controls that rate: smaller exponents give more probability to very large observations. Doubling any threshold in its support multiplies the survival probability by 2−α2^{-\alpha}2−α.

Basic probability and integration are enough for this page. A survival probability means P(X>x)\mathbb P(X>x)P(X>x). The formulas below define the CDF FFF, density fff, and quantile QQQ; the survival table illustrates the exponent.

Shape and tail intuition¶

Above its lower cutoff, the Pareto law has no characteristic upper scale. Once both thresholds lie in the Pareto tail, multiplying the threshold by a fixed factor changes the exceedance probability by a fixed power:

Fˉ(tx)Fˉ(x)=t−α,t>0,  x≥xm,  tx≥xm.\frac{\bar F(tx)}{\bar F(x)}=t^{-\alpha},\qquad t>0,\; x\ge x_m,\; tx\ge x_m.Fˉ(x)Fˉ(tx)​=t−α,t>0,x≥xm​,tx≥xm​.
(6)

Smaller α\alphaα gives a larger exceedance probability at every x>xmx>x_mx>xm​. The consequences for sums depend on whether the sample size or the exceedance threshold grows; moment existence also depends on the moment order.

The density also shows how the distribution piles mass near the lower cutoff while leaving a long right tail. Its values and scaling make this explicit.

At the cutoff, f(xm)=α/xmf(x_m)=\alpha/x_mf(xm​)=α/xm​. For x≥xmx\ge x_mx≥xm​,

f(2x)f(x)=2−(α+1).\frac{f(2x)}{f(x)}=2^{-(\alpha+1)}.f(x)f(2x)​=2−(α+1).
(7)

This ratio describes the decay of the density without a numerical plot.

Larger α\alphaα concentrates more mass near xmx_mxm​ and makes the density decay faster. Smaller α\alphaα keeps more visible mass far from the cutoff.

Moment thresholds¶

For a positive moment order ppp, the exact Pareto formula is

E[Xp]=αxmpα−p(p<α),\mathbb E[X^p]=\frac{\alpha x_m^p}{\alpha-p}\quad (p<\alpha),E[Xp]=α−pαxmp​​(p<α),
(10)

and the moment is infinite for p≥αp\ge\alphap≥α. The Pareto moment theorem gives the direct integration proof and the truncated-moment formulas and table. Truncation makes each truncated population moment finite: raising the upper cutoff reveals whether it approaches a limit, grows logarithmically at p=αp=\alphap=α, or grows as a power for p>αp>\alphap>α.

For a general regularly varying tail, Fˉ(x)=x−αL(x)\bar F(x)=x^{-\alpha}L(x)Fˉ(x)=x−αL(x), the boundary p=αp=\alphap=α depends on LLL. Karamata’s theorem explains this distinction; the exact Pareto boundary always diverges.

The moment-existence thresholds give a concrete example:

With xm=1x_m=1xm​=1 and α=1.16\alpha=1.16α=1.16, the mean is 1.16/(1.16−1)=7.251.16/(1.16-1)=7.251.16/(1.16−1)=7.25 and the second raw moment is infinite; the variance is therefore infinite.

For simulation, inverse transform sampling gives

(11)

When xmx_mxm​ is known and the observations are independent and identically distributed exact Pareto draws, the maximum-likelihood estimator is

α^=n∑i=1nlog⁡(Xi/xm).\widehat\alpha=\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^n\log(X_i/x_m)}.α=∑i=1n​log(Xi​/xm​)n​.

This formula requires a positive denominator, which holds almost surely under the continuous model. If every observation equals xmx_mxm​, the likelihood has no finite maximizer; it increases as α→∞\alpha\to\inftyα→∞.

This estimator is not a license to fit an exact Pareto from the sample minimum: empirical tail thresholds are rarely known and must be diagnosed separately.

The symmetric shifted double Pareto model extends the tail construction to signed observations.

Sums and moment orders¶

Let X1,X2,…X_1,X_2,\ldotsX1​,X2​,… be iid Pareto Type I variables with the same α>0\alpha>0α>0 and xm>0x_m>0xm​>0, and put Sn=∑i=1nXiS_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_iSn​=∑i=1n​Xi​ and Mn=max⁡1≤i≤nXiM_n=\max_{1\le i\le n}X_iMn​=max1≤i≤n​Xi​. For a growing sample, the max-to-sum ratio has three regimes:

  • If α>1\alpha>1α>1, then Mn/Sn→0M_n/S_n\to0Mn​/Sn​→0 almost surely. The linked finite-mean proof gives Sn/n→E[X]>0S_n/n\to\mathbb E[X]>0Sn​/n→E[X]>0 and Mn/n→0M_n/n\to0Mn​/n→0 almost surely, so their quotient tends to zero.

  • If 0<α<10<\alpha<10<α<1, then Mn/Sn→dRαM_n/S_n\xrightarrow{d}R_\alphaMn​/Sn​d​Rα​, where RαR_\alphaRα​ has a nondegenerate distribution on (0,1)(0,1)(0,1). Kevei and Mason (2014), p. 632 restate the iid result and give the corresponding sum/maximum limit law; our ratio is its reciprocal.

  • If α=1\alpha=1α=1, the mean is infinite but Mn/Sn→0M_n/S_n\to0Mn​/Sn​→0 in probability. More precisely, Mn/(xmn)M_n/(x_m n)Mn​/(xm​n) is bounded in probability and Sn/(xmnlog⁡n)→1S_n/(x_m n\log n)\to1Sn​/(xm​nlogn)→1 in probability. The calculation below proves these exact-Pareto assertions; it does not assert almost-sure convergence of the ratio.

For every α>0\alpha>0α>0, a different limit holds when the integer n≥1n\ge1n≥1 is fixed and the threshold grows:

P(Sn>x)∼P(Mn>x)∼nP(X>x),x→∞.\mathbb P(S_n>x)\sim\mathbb P(M_n>x) \sim n\mathbb P(X>x),\qquad x\to\infty.P(Sn​>x)∼P(Mn​>x)∼nP(X>x),x→∞.

This is the subexponential rare-event statement: a fixed-size sum exceeds a sufficiently high threshold mainly when one summand exceeds it. It holds even when α>1\alpha>1α>1 and the largest share vanishes along growing samples. Caveats¶

  • Empirical data rarely follows an exact Pareto law from its minimum value. A tail model needs a threshold choice, diagnostic plots, and sensitivity checks.

  • A finite theoretical mean can still be practically hard to estimate when α\alphaα is close to 1. The issue is pre-asymptotic behavior, not merely the formal existence of E[X]\mathbb E[X]E[X].

  • The exact failure mode depends on the moment order. At p=αp=\alphap=α, the cutoff moment grows like log⁡b\log blogb; for p>αp>\alphap>α, it grows like bp−αb^{p-\alpha}bp−α.

  • Do not estimate high moments of heavy-tailed samples without checking whether those moments are implied by the fitted tail exponent.
