Pickands-Balkema-de Haan Theorem

Threshold intuition¶

Block-maxima theory says that properly normalized maxima have only a small number of possible limiting shapes, collected in the Generalized Extreme-Value Distribution. The Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem says the matching threshold view has the same discipline: once we condition on being far enough into the tail, the remaining excess has an approximately Generalized Pareto Distribution.

For fat-tail work, the case ξ>0\xi>0ξ>0 is the main bridge. It corresponds to a regularly varying right tail with exponent α=1/ξ\alpha=1/\xiα=1/ξ. The theorem does not say every high observation is generated by a clean Pareto law. It says the excess distribution over moving high thresholds has a universal limit shape under the same domain-of-attraction assumptions used in extreme-value theory.

Exact Pareto excess special case¶

The full Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem is cited here, not proved. The calculation below proves the exact Pareto special case: once the threshold is above the lower cutoff, the excess distribution is already generalized Pareto, not merely asymptotically close to it.

The full theorem is a classical result of Balkema and de Haan and, independently, Pickands. Its proof uses the equivalence between convergence of normalized maxima and convergence of normalized threshold excesses. We record the statement and use exact Pareto algebra as a checkable special case.

If XXX is Pareto Type I with lower cutoff xmx_mxm​ and tail exponent α\alphaα, then for u≥xm>0u\ge x_m>0u≥xm​>0, α>0\alpha>0α>0, and y≥0y\ge0y≥0,

P(X−u>y∣X>u)=P(X>u+y)P(X>u)=(1+yu)−α.\begin{gathered} P(X-u>y\mid X>u)\\ =\frac{P(X>u+y)}{P(X>u)}\\ =\left(1+\frac{y}{u}\right)^{-\alpha}. \end{gathered}P(X−u>y∣X>u)=P(X>u)P(X>u+y)​=(1+uy​)−α.​
(5)

This is exactly a generalized Pareto survival function with

ξ=1α,β(u)=uα.\xi=\frac1\alpha, \qquad \beta(u)=\frac{u}{\alpha}.ξ=α1​,β(u)=αu​.
(6)

Thus exact Pareto tails do not merely approach the generalized Pareto form; they have it at every threshold above xmx_mxm​.

What the limit permits¶

SCoFT v4 §9.1.3, pp. 176–177, introduces conditional excesses Taleb, 2025. In the theorem above, membership in a maximum domain of attraction and an absolute CDF difference are essential. Approaching the endpoint of an arbitrary CDF does not by itself establish a GPD limit. The full theorem is cited from Balkema & Haan (1974) and Pickands (1975); this page proves only special cases and consequences.

For a standard normal parent, excesses also have a limit, but on a shrinking scale. For u→∞u\to\inftyu→∞ and fixed y≥0y\ge0y≥0, the normal-tail bounds in the Gaussian maxima proof give

(7)

Thus ξ=0\xi=0ξ=0 with asymptotic excess scale 1/u1/u1/u, whereas exact Pareto has ξ=1/α\xi=1/\alphaξ=1/α and scale u/αu/\alphau/α. A bounded example is uniform on [0,b][0,b][0,b], b>0b>0b>0: above 0≤u<b0\le u<b0≤u<b its excess is exactly uniform on [0,b−u][0,b-u][0,b−u], a GPD with ξ=−1\xi=-1ξ=−1 and β(u)=b−u\beta(u)=b-uβ(u)=b−u.

These three cases can be compared with generalized_pareto. The normal row is a limit comparison; the other two are exact.

for xi, scale in ((0, 1), (.5, .5), (-1, 1)):
    print(f"GPD xi={xi:g}: sf(1)={generalized_pareto(xi, beta=scale).sf(1):.4g}")

Uniform closeness of CDFs does not control unbounded moments: the contamination counterexample shows why. For the excesses of one fixed parent in a maximum domain of attraction, however, the tail structure supplies the required control when ξ<1\xi<1ξ<1. This gives the following stronger conclusion.

Numerical check¶

For a standard normal, integrate the scaled excess survival directly, using log survivals to avoid dividing tiny probabilities. The ratio tends to the unit-scale exponential mean returned by generalized_pareto. The same quadrature checks the exact GPD cases with shapes 1/21/21/2 and -1. These few numerical values illustrate the corollary; they do not prove it or test extreme-parameter accuracy.

Code and output: mean-excess limits
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad
from scipy.stats import norm
from xmath.incerto.distributions import generalized_pareto

target = generalized_pareto(0, beta=1).mean()
for u in (2, 4, 8):
    ratio = quad(lambda z: np.exp(norm.logsf(u+z/u)-norm.logsf(u)),
                 0, np.inf)[0]
    print(f"normal u={u}: e/beta={ratio:.4g}, limit={target:g}")
for xi in (.5, -1):
    law = generalized_pareto(xi, beta=1)
    ratio = quad(law.sf, 0, law.support()[1])[0]
    print(f"exact GPD xi={xi:g}: e/beta={ratio:.4g}, mean={law.mean():.4g}")

Output

normal u=4: e/beta=0.9024, limit=1
normal u=8: e/beta=0.9709, limit=1
exact GPD xi=0.5: e/beta=2, mean=2
exact GPD xi=-1: e/beta=0.5, mean=0.5

Caveats¶

  • The theorem is asymptotic in the threshold. It does not choose the threshold for a finite dataset.

  • Dependence, seasonality, volatility clustering, rounding, truncation, and mixtures can all distort threshold exceedances.

  • A generalized Pareto fit is a tail model, not a proof that the full distribution is Pareto.

  • When ξ≥1\xi\ge1ξ≥1, the fitted generalized Pareto model has no finite mean. When ξ≥1/2\xi\ge1/2ξ≥1/2, it has no finite variance. These moment boundaries are often the practical reason the shape estimate matters.

  • Raising the threshold can reduce tail-approximation bias but leaves fewer exceedances; it does not guarantee a monotone improvement in a fitted model.
