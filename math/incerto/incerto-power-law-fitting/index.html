Fitting a power-law tail

A fitted power law answers a conditional question: which exponent best describes observations above a proposed lower threshold? It does not establish that the tail is a power law. Clauset, Shalizi, and Newman (CSN) Clauset et al. (2009) combine maximum likelihood with a threshold search; goodness-of-fit and model comparisons then examine the resulting model.

This page develops those calculations for independent, identically distributed positive observations. The Pareto distribution supplies the exact continuous model. The Hill estimator allows a broader asymptotic interpretation under regular variation, while threshold diagnostics explain why choosing the fitting region matters.

Worked example

The tech layoffs example applies these methods to event counts and workforce log losses. The examples on this theory page remain synthetic.

Two exponent conventions¶

We retain the shared notation: α>0\alpha>0α>0 is the survival-tail exponent and ξ=1/α\xi=1/\alphaξ=1/α is the positive extreme-value index. Write u>0u>0u>0 for the fitting threshold (the code calls it xmin). On this page, aaa is a local symbol for the density or mass exponent used by CSN:

a=α+1,Fˉ(x∣X≥u)=(x/u)−α,x≥u.\begin{aligned} a&=\alpha+1,\\ \bar F(x\mid X\ge u)&=(x/u)^{-\alpha},\qquad x\ge u. \end{aligned}aFˉ(x∣X≥u)​=α+1,=(x/u)−α,x≥u.​
(1)

Every alpha argument or result in the package below uses α\alphaα, including for integer data. For an integer power law the survival is a zeta ratio, not exactly (x/u)−α(x/u)^{-\alpha}(x/u)−α; its large-xxx exponent is still α=a−1\alpha=a-1α=a−1. The change of exponent convention must precede any comparison with software that reports aaa.

Let nnn denote the full sample size and kkk the number of observations satisfying xi≥ux_i\ge uxi​≥u. At each cutoff we reindex those retained values as x1,…,xkx_1,\ldots,x_kx1​,…,xk​ for the likelihood sums. The count k=k(u)k=k(u)k=k(u) varies with the cutoff. Equality is included, which matters for integer data and for thresholds chosen from observations; kkk need not be the strict exceedance count. The likelihood formulas require k≥1k\ge1k≥1; the numerical helpers require at least two retained values and report the retained count as n_tail.

Continuous likelihood and its relation to Hill¶

At a fixed threshold, the conditional density with respect to Lebesgue measure is

f(x∣α,u)=αu(x/u)−(α+1),x≥u.\begin{aligned} f(x\mid\alpha,u)&=\frac{\alpha}{u}(x/u)^{-(\alpha+1)},\\ &\qquad x\ge u. \end{aligned}f(x∣α,u)​=uα​(x/u)−(α+1),x≥u.​
(2)

We derive the MLE within this exact model. Let T=∑i=1klog⁡(xi/u)T=\sum_{i=1}^k\log(x_i/u)T=∑i=1k​log(xi​/u). Its log likelihood and score are

ℓ(α)=klog⁡α−klog⁡u−(α+1)T,ℓ′(α)=k/α−T,ℓ′′(α)=−k/α2.\begin{aligned} \ell(\alpha)&=k\log\alpha-k\log u-(\alpha+1)T,\\ \ell'(\alpha)&=k/\alpha-T,\\ \ell''(\alpha)&=-k/\alpha^2. \end{aligned}ℓ(α)ℓ′(α)ℓ′′(α)​=klogα−klogu−(α+1)T,=k/α−T,=−k/α2.​
(3)

If T>0T>0T>0, strict concavity gives a unique finite maximum:

α^=k/T,a^=1+k/T.\begin{aligned} \widehat\alpha&=k/T,\\ \widehat a&=1+k/T. \end{aligned}αa​=k/T,=1+k/T.​
(4)

If every retained value equals uuu, the likelihood increases without a finite maximizer as α→∞\alpha\to\inftyα→∞. Reporting a finite exponent in that case would invent information about the tail’s spread.

The reciprocal estimate is the average log excess used by Hill Hill, 1975. There is a small but real counting distinction. For distinct continuous observations with ascending order statistics x1:n,…,xn:nx_{1:n},\ldots,x_{n:n}x1:n​,…,xn:n​, a Hill fit above xn−k:nx_{n-k:n}xn−k:n​ uses the kkk larger observations, where 1≤k<n1\le k<n1≤k<n. In this comparison, kkk counts Hill’s upper observations. Setting u=xn−k:nu=x_{n-k:n}u=xn−k:n​ and retaining equality in the CSN fit gives k+1k+1k+1 observations, including one zero log excess. Thus

α^inclusive=k+1kα^Hill.\widehat\alpha_{\mathrm{inclusive}} =\frac{k+1}{k}\widehat\alpha_{\mathrm{Hill}}.αinclusive​=kk+1​αHill​.
(5)

With a fixed external cutoff and the same retained sample, the reciprocal mean-log-excess calculation is identical. Ties require the actual counts; the displayed factor assumes a single threshold observation.

Use sample_powerlaw, powerlaw_mle_continuous, and powerlaw_standard_error:

Integer data: the zeta likelihood¶

For positive integer observations and integer u≥1u\ge1u≥1, specify a mass function with respect to counting measure:

p(x∣a,u)=x−aζ(a,u),x∈{u,u+1,…},ζ(a,u)=∑j=u∞j−a,a>1.\begin{aligned} p(x\mid a,u)&=\frac{x^{-a}}{\zeta(a,u)},\qquad x\in\{u,u+1,\ldots\},\\ \zeta(a,u)&=\sum_{j=u}^{\infty}j^{-a},\qquad a>1. \end{aligned}p(x∣a,u)ζ(a,u)​=ζ(a,u)x−a​,x∈{u,u+1,…},=j=u∑∞​j−a,a>1.​
(6)

A Yule-type mechanism can produce an asymptotic power tail without producing this exact finite-threshold mass function. The zeta model is a hypothesis to check, even when the data are integers and look heavy-tailed.

Writing primes for derivatives in aaa, the score equation is

ℓ(a)=−klog⁡ζ(a,u)−a∑i=1klog⁡xi,ζ′(a^,u)ζ(a^,u)=−1k∑i=1klog⁡xi.\begin{aligned} \ell(a)&=-k\log\zeta(a,u)-a\sum_{i=1}^k\log x_i,\\ \frac{\zeta'(\widehat a,u)}{\zeta(\widehat a,u)} &=-\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^k\log x_i. \end{aligned}ℓ(a)ζ(a,u)ζ′(a,u)​​=−klogζ(a,u)−ai=1∑k​logxi​,=−k1​i=1∑k​logxi​.​
(7)

This generally needs a numerical solve. The reason the objective is concave also supplies its information formula. Differentiating the normalized series for a>1a>1a>1 gives

∂alog⁡ζ(a,u)=−Ea[log⁡X],∂a2log⁡ζ(a,u)=Var⁡a(log⁡X)>0.\begin{aligned} \partial_a\log\zeta(a,u)&=-\mathbb E_a[\log X],\\ \partial_a^2\log\zeta(a,u)&=\operatorname{Var}_a(\log X)>0. \end{aligned}∂a​logζ(a,u)∂a2​logζ(a,u)​=−Ea​[logX],=Vara​(logX)>0.​
(8)

Logarithmic moments are finite at every interior a>1a>1a>1; this calculation does not require EX\mathbb E XEX or Var⁡(X)\operatorname{Var}(X)Var(X) to exist. The likelihood is strictly concave. Its score crosses zero when the sample mean of log⁡X\log XlogX exceeds log⁡u\log ulogu; an all-uuu tail again has only an infinite-exponent limit.

The half-integer approximation¶

Replacing the integer sum by a continuous integral starting halfway below the first integer suggests

a^approx=1+k∑i=1klog⁡ ⁣(xi/(u−12)).\widehat a_{\mathrm{approx}} =1+\frac{k}{\sum_{i=1}^k\log\!\left(x_i/(u-\tfrac12)\right)}.aapprox​=1+∑i=1k​log(xi​/(u−21​))k​.
(9)

The half-unit correction matters: substituting uuu in this approximation is a different and generally worse approximation. Neither expression is the exact zeta MLE. When uuu is small or aaa is large, a few lattice points carry much of the mass, so replacing their sum by an integral can distort the normalization substantially. Larger uuu helps only relative to the local rate of decay. There is no universal cutoff beyond which the approximation is accurate for every exponent. A large sample reduces sampling error while leaving this approximation error in place.

powerlaw_mle_discrete uses the zeta likelihood by default. Its optional approximation returns the survival exponent a^approx−1\widehat a_{\mathrm{approx}}-1aapprox​−1. Here we compare both on exact zeta samples with the same population exponent:

from xmath.incerto.powerlaw import sample_powerlaw, powerlaw_mle_discrete

for u in (1, 8):
    x = sample_powerlaw(2, u, 2000, discrete=True, random_state=320+u)
    exact = powerlaw_mle_discrete(x, u)
    approx = powerlaw_mle_discrete(x, u, approximate=True)
    print(f"u={u}: exact alpha={exact:.3f}; approximate alpha={approx:.3f}")

Output

u=1: exact alpha=2.032; approximate alpha=1.173
u=8: exact alpha=2.059; approximate alpha=2.043

Choose a threshold by refitting at each candidate¶

For each admissible threshold uuu, fit its own exponent and compute the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance between the conditional empirical CDF Fk,uF_{k,u}Fk,u​ and the fitted model CDF Fα^(u),uF_{\widehat\alpha(u),u}Fα(u),u​:

D(u)=sup⁡x≥u∣Fk,u(x)−Fα^(u),u(x)∣,u^∈arg⁡min⁡u∈UD(u).\begin{aligned} D(u)&=\sup_{x\ge u} \left|F_{k,u}(x)-F_{\widehat\alpha(u),u}(x)\right|,\\ \widehat u&\in\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal U}D(u). \end{aligned}D(u)u​=x≥usup​∣∣​Fk,u​(x)−Fα(u),u​(x)∣∣​,∈argu∈Umin​D(u).​
(10)

Here U\mathcal UU is the declared candidate set. A usual choice is the distinct observed values with enough observations above them to permit a fit. A fixed grid can reduce computation, but then the result minimizes over that grid only. Record the minimum tail count, candidate rule, selected count, and nearby values of D(u)D(u)D(u); a small difference between two minima is not evidence for a sharply located population boundary.

For continuous data, the supremum checks both sides of every empirical jump. For integer data the model jumps too:

Fa,u(x)=1−ζ(a,x+1)ζ(a,u),x∈{u,u+1,…}.F_{a,u}(x)=1-\frac{\zeta(a,x+1)}{\zeta(a,u)},\qquad x\in\{u,u+1,\ldots\}.Fa,u​(x)=1−ζ(a,u)ζ(a,x+1)​,x∈{u,u+1,…}.
(11)

Check x−1x-1x−1 and xxx for every observed integer xxx. These endpoints also cover gaps containing no observations: the empirical CDF is constant there and the model CDF is monotone. Comparing just the right sides at observed values can miss the largest discrepancy. Use ks_distance_powerlaw and select_xmin.

Fixed-threshold errors and jointly refitted uncertainty¶

For the exact continuous law at fixed uuu, log⁡(X/u)\log(X/u)log(X/u) is exponential with rate α\alphaα. Consequently the per-observation Fisher information is 1/α21/\alpha^21/α2. The ordinary interior-MLE approximation gives

se⁡(α^)≃α^k,se⁡(a^)≃a^−1k.\begin{aligned} \operatorname{se}(\widehat\alpha)&\simeq\frac{\widehat\alpha}{\sqrt k},\\ \operatorname{se}(\widehat a)&\simeq\frac{\widehat a-1}{\sqrt k}. \end{aligned}se(α)se(a)​≃k​α​,≃k​a−1​.​
(12)

The second line is the familiar CSN expression, often written (α^CSN−1)/k(\widehat\alpha_{\mathrm{CSN}}-1)/\sqrt k(αCSN​−1)/k​. Adding one changes the exponent’s name and level, but not its standard error. This is asymptotic sampling error conditional on the threshold and retained count, not a finite-sample unbiased variance formula.

For the discrete model, the same MLE approximation uses

I(a;u)=ζ′′(a,u)ζ(a,u)−[ζ′(a,u)ζ(a,u)]2,se⁡(a^)≃[kI(a^;u)]−1/2.\begin{aligned} I(a;u)&=\frac{\zeta''(a,u)}{\zeta(a,u)} -\left[\frac{\zeta'(a,u)}{\zeta(a,u)}\right]^2,\\ \operatorname{se}(\widehat a)&\simeq[kI(\widehat a;u)]^{-1/2}. \end{aligned}I(a;u)se(a)​=ζ(a,u)ζ′′(a,u)​−[ζ(a,u)ζ′(a,u)​]2,≃[kI(a;u)]−1/2.​
(13)

The variance identity above derives III; asymptotic normality and the MLE standard-error approximation are cited results, not proved here Clauset et al., 2009, §3.1 and appendix B. The helper evaluates the discrete curvature numerically. Very steep laws or extreme numerical scales can make that calculation ill-conditioned.

When uuu was selected from the data, both formulas omit selection uncertainty. For an empirical bootstrap, resample nnn observations with replacement from the whole sample, then repeat the threshold search and exponent fit. Retain the pairs (α^∗(b),u^∗(b))(\widehat\alpha^{*(b)},\widehat u^{*(b)})(α∗(b),u∗(b)). Their sample standard deviations estimate the two marginal errors; their covariance records how the estimates move together. Resampling only the selected tail at a fixed cutoff cannot measure uncertainty in choosing that cutoff.

powerlaw_bootstrap_se performs this full refit. The small replicate count here illustrates the calculation; it is insufficient for stable interval endpoints.

from xmath.incerto.powerlaw import sample_powerlaw, powerlaw_bootstrap_se

x = sample_powerlaw(1.6, 2, 240, random_state=330)
boot = powerlaw_bootstrap_se(x, replicates=24, min_tail=40, random_state=331)
print(f"Bootstrap SE(alpha): {boot['alpha_se']:.3f}")
print(f"Bootstrap SE(xmin): {boot['xmin_se']:.3f}")

Output

Bootstrap SE(alpha): 0.126
Bootstrap SE(xmin): 0.185

The threshold selector is discontinuous and may jump between competing minima. Bootstrap standard deviations are useful sensitivity summaries; they do not by themselves establish confidence-interval coverage. Inspect the paired replicates and repeat the analysis under reasonable candidate rules. This empirical bootstrap is different from the fitted-null bootstrap used for goodness of fit.

Practical limits¶

An exact power tail above some finite threshold is stronger than regular variation. A slowly varying correction may remain detectable at every available threshold. Raising the cutoff can hide that discrepancy by leaving little data; report the retained fraction alongside the fit.

Rounded continuous measurements need a measurement model; integer support alone does not make the zeta mass correct. Censoring, reporting floors, and a hard upper limit change the likelihood. A multiplicative change of continuous units preserves the fitted exponent when the cutoff changes with it, whereas adding a constant changes the model. Integer units fix the lattice and cannot be rescaled arbitrarily while preserving the zeta family.

The likelihood and resampling calculations assume iid observations. Dependence requires an appropriate sampling model or resampling design. Least-squares slopes of logged histograms do not reproduce these likelihoods: binning changes weights, empty bins vanish, and the errors are not those of ordinary linear regression. Finally, a small fitted KS distance needs its own calibration. It is a discrepancy statistic, not a p-value.
