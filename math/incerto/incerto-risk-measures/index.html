VaR, expected shortfall, and tail uncertainty

A loss quantile specifies a threshold. Expected shortfall averages the losses in an upper probability slice. Both depend on a probability law; reporting either quantity without its model and estimation assumptions leaves the uncertainty unresolved.

SCoFT v4, chapter 13, §13.3, pp. 257–258 Taleb, 2025, motivates the error-propagation question. Its random-probability calculation is derived in probability conflation. Here we define the risk measures precisely and prove additional results for a random Pareto exponent. These two uncertainty models are distinct.

Quantiles and expected shortfall¶

Let X≥0X\ge0X≥0 be a finite almost surely loss, with CDF FFF and survival Fˉ\bar FFˉ as in the notation canon. Use 0<τ<10<\tau<10<τ<1 for confidence and p=1−τp=1-\taup=1−τ for tail mass; α\alphaα remains the tail exponent. Define

qτ=inf⁡{x:F(x)≥τ},VaR⁡τ(X)=qτ,ES⁡τ(X)=1p∫τ1qs ds.\begin{gathered} q_\tau=\inf\{x:F(x)\ge\tau\},\\ \operatorname{VaR}_\tau(X)=q_\tau,\\ \operatorname{ES}_\tau(X) =\frac1p\int_\tau^1 q_s\,ds. \end{gathered}qτ​=inf{x:F(x)≥τ},VaRτ​(X)=qτ​,ESτ​(X)=p1​∫τ1​qs​ds.​
(1)

The quantile is finite for each interior confidence level. The integral may be infinite. ES selects the upper mass ppp, including the required fraction of an atom at the quantile. Consequently the strict conditional mean E[X∣X>qτ]\mathbb E[X\mid X>q_\tau]E[X∣X>qτ​] is not a general definition of ES.

We prove the equivalent excess-payoff formula, which also handles atoms. Represent the loss by its quantile function evaluated at a uniform random variable; this has CDF FFF. Monotonicity gives qs≤qτq_s\le q_\tauqs​≤qτ​ below τ\tauτ and qs≥qτq_s\ge q_\tauqs​≥qτ​ above τ\tauτ, so

E[(X−qτ)+]=∫τ1(qs−qτ) ds,ES⁡τ(X)=qτ+E[(X−qτ)+]p.\begin{gathered} \mathbb E[(X-q_\tau)_+]=\int_\tau^1(q_s-q_\tau)\,ds,\\ \operatorname{ES}_\tau(X)=q_\tau+\frac{\mathbb E[(X-q_\tau)_+]}p. \end{gathered}E[(X−qτ​)+​]=∫τ1​(qs​−qτ​)ds,ESτ​(X)=qτ​+pE[(X−qτ​)+​]​.​
(2)

These are nonnegative integrals and remain valid with infinity. If the law is continuous at qτq_\tauqτ​ with P(X>qτ)=pP(X>q_\tau)=pP(X>qτ​)=p, ES equals the conditional tail mean. The unconditional tail-level quantity from the calibration note is then E[X1{X>qτ}]=pES⁡τ(X)\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_{\{X>q_\tau\}}]=p\operatorname{ES}_\tau(X)E[X1{X>qτ​}​]=pESτ​(X), not ES itself. At an atom, the exact expression is

pES⁡τ(X)=E[X1{X>qτ}]+qτ{p−P(X>qτ)}.\begin{aligned} p\operatorname{ES}_\tau(X) &=\mathbb E[X\mathbf1_{\{X>q_\tau\}}]\\ &\quad+q_\tau\{p-P(X>q_\tau)\}. \end{aligned}pESτ​(X)​=E[X1{X>qτ​}​]+qτ​{p−P(X>qτ​)}.​
(3)

Use discrete_risk. Here the worst tenth of the law includes all the losses of 20 and some losses of 5. Averaging only observations strictly above the quantile would give a different answer.

from xmath.incerto.risk_measures import discrete_risk

risk = discrete_risk([0, 5, 20], [.85, .1, .05], .1)
print(f"VaR={risk['var']:.1f}, ES={risk['es']:.1f}")

Output

VaR=5.0, ES=12.5

VaR is also a minimizer of expected quantile loss when the first moment is finite, as proved in the point-forecast note. Use quantile_loss and finite_payoff_mean to compare three candidate thresholds under this same finite law.

    risk = finite_payoff_mean([.85, .1, .05], losses)
    print(f"threshold={threshold}: expected quantile loss={risk:.2f}")

threshold=5: expected quantile loss=1.10

A quantile can fail subadditivity¶

Consider mutually exclusive losses: (X,Y)(X,Y)(X,Y) takes (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) with probability 0.92, (10,0)(10,0)(10,0) with probability 0.04, and (0,10)(0,10)(0,10) with probability 0.04. At confidence 0.95, each marginal has VaR zero, while X+YX+YX+Y has VaR 10. This finite example proves that VaR is not subadditive over all joint loss laws. It is not a claim that every portfolio violates subadditivity.

The quantile definition also applies to real integrable losses. On that domain, ES is subadditive regardless of dependence. We prove this from the excess formula. Within this proof, for any real ttt, let

JX(t)=t+E[(X−t)+]p.J_X(t)=t+\frac{\mathbb E[(X-t)_+]}p.JX​(t)=t+pE[(X−t)+​]​.
(4)

Integrating qs−t≤(qs−t)+q_s-t\le(q_s-t)_+qs​−t≤(qs​−t)+​ over the upper slice gives ES⁡τ(X)≤JX(t)\operatorname{ES}_\tau(X)\le J_X(t)ESτ​(X)≤JX​(t). Equality holds at t=qτt=q_\taut=qτ​, so ES is the minimum of JXJ_XJX​. The pointwise inequality

(X+Y−t−s)+≤(X−t)++(Y−s)+(X+Y-t-s)_+ \le(X-t)_++(Y-s)_+(X+Y−t−s)+​≤(X−t)+​+(Y−s)+​
(5)

implies JX+Y(t+s)≤JX(t)+JY(s)J_{X+Y}(t+s)\le J_X(t)+J_Y(s)JX+Y​(t+s)≤JX​(t)+JY​(s). Choose marginal quantile minimizers and minimize the left side to obtain

ES⁡τ(X+Y)≤ES⁡τ(X)+ES⁡τ(Y).\operatorname{ES}_\tau(X+Y) \le\operatorname{ES}_\tau(X)+\operatorname{ES}_\tau(Y).ESτ​(X+Y)≤ESτ​(X)+ESτ​(Y).
(6)

Monotonicity, positive homogeneity, and translation by a constant follow directly from the quantile definition, within the integrable loss domain. These properties explain the coherence of ES. They do not bound the sampling error of an estimated ES, nor make an infinite ES finite.

from xmath.incerto.risk_measures import discrete_risk

    r = discrete_risk(x, [.92, .04, .04], .05)
    print(f"{name}: VaR={r['var']:.1f}, ES={r['es']:.1f}")

Output

X: VaR=0.0, ES=8.0
Y: VaR=0.0, ES=8.0
X+Y: VaR=10.0, ES=10.0

Exact Pareto risk and its moment boundary¶

For X∼Pareto⁡(α,xm)X\sim\operatorname{Pareto}(\alpha,x_m)X∼Pareto(α,xm​) with α>0\alpha>0α>0, xm>0x_m>0xm​>0, inversion of Fˉ(x)=(xm/x)α\bar F(x)=(x_m/x)^\alphaFˉ(x)=(xm​/x)α gives

qτ=xmp−1/α,ES⁡τ(X)=αα−1qτ,α>1.\begin{gathered} q_\tau=x_m p^{-1/\alpha},\\ \operatorname{ES}_\tau(X) =\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}q_\tau,\\ \alpha>1. \end{gathered}qτ​=xm​p−1/α,ESτ​(X)=α−1α​qτ​,α>1.​
(7)

To prove the second line, integrate the Pareto survival above qτq_\tauqτ​: E[(X−qτ)+]=pqτ/(α−1)\mathbb E[(X-q_\tau)_+]=pq_\tau/(\alpha-1)E[(X−qτ​)+​]=pqτ​/(α−1), then substitute into the excess formula. For 0<α≤10<\alpha\le10<α≤1 this integral diverges, hence ES is infinite at every interior confidence level, while VaR remains finite. These are exact-Pareto statements. The equality case for a general regularly varying law can depend on its slowly varying factor; see moment existence.

Mixing distributions is a different operation¶

Now specify X∣A∼Pareto⁡(A,xm)X\mid A\sim\operatorname{Pareto}(A,x_m)X∣A∼Pareto(A,xm​) with the same fixed xmx_mxm​ in every component, A>0A>0A>0 almost surely, and finite a0=EAa_0=\mathbb E Aa0​=EA. For x>xmx>x_mx>xm​, convexity of a↦(xm/x)aa\mapsto(x_m/x)^aa↦(xm​/x)a proves

Fˉmix(x)=E[(xm/x)A],Fˉmix(x)≥(xm/x)a0.\begin{gathered} \bar F_{\rm mix}(x)=\mathbb E[(x_m/x)^A],\\ \bar F_{\rm mix}(x)\ge(x_m/x)^{a_0}. \end{gathered}Fˉmix​(x)=E[(xm​/x)A],Fˉmix​(x)≥(xm​/x)a0​.​
(11)

This is first-order stochastic dominance over the Pareto law at a0a_0a0​. It implies larger or equal quantiles at every confidence level, and larger or equal ES by integration of quantiles. For nonconstant AAA, strict Jensen and continuous strictly decreasing mixture survival give a strict quantile increase at every interior confidence level. The inequality is specific to this common-cutoff exponent mixture. Holding a median or mean fixed by changing xmx_mxm​ is a different comparison.

The mixture’s VaR is finite: dominated convergence gives E[(xm/x)A]→0\mathbb E[(x_m/x)^A]\to0E[(xm​/x)A]→0 as x→∞x\to\inftyx→∞. Its mean and ES can still be infinite. With AAA uniform on (1,2)(1,2)(1,2), Tonelli gives an infinite mixture mean, hence infinite ES; truncating the loss below any finite quantile cannot remove that infinite tail mean.

There is also a sharp distinction between mixture VaR and average model VaR. With AAA uniform on (0,1)(0,1)(0,1), E[p−1/A]=∞\mathbb E[p^{-1/A}]=\inftyE[p−1/A]=∞ for every 0<p<10<p<10<p<1, while mixture VaR stays finite. The first claim follows by substituting z=1/Az=1/Az=1/A: the resulting integral of p−z/z2p^{-z}/z^2p−z/z2 diverges. No identity interchanges averaging a quantile with mixing laws.

Estimation adds another source of uncertainty¶

The Hill estimator estimates the extreme-value index ξ=1/α\xi=1/\alphaξ=1/α from a chosen number kkk of upper log spacings. Threshold choice and finite-sample error are additional assumptions. In an exact Pareto model the fixed-kkk reciprocal estimate is biased; Jensen at its mean therefore does not by itself compare its plug-in risk to the true risk. Nor does a distribution of repeated fitted parameters automatically become a posterior mixture for future losses.

The example reuses hill_alpha_estimator and pareto_risk. It uses a known synthetic cutoff of one, so it isolates exponent error instead of estimating the cutoff as well. At every kkk, it retains fits with infinite ES. The intervals summarize sampling variation; they are not confidence intervals.

Repeated fixed-k estimates
import numpy as np
from xmath.incerto.estimators import hill_alpha_estimator
from xmath.incerto.risk_measures import pareto_risk

rng = np.random.default_rng(1307)
samples = 1 + rng.pareto(1.2, (400, 250))
for k in (25, 100):
    alphas = [hill_alpha_estimator(x, k) for x in samples]
    risks = [pareto_risk(a, .01) for a in alphas]
    lo, mid, hi = np.quantile([r['var'] for r in risks], [.1, .5, .9])
    infinite = sum(np.isinf(r['es']) for r in risks)
    print(f"k={k}: VaR 10/50/90%={lo:.1f}/{mid:.1f}/{hi:.1f}; infinite ES={infinite}/400")

Output

k=25: VaR 10/50/90%=16.9/45.4/131.3; infinite ES=66/400
k=100: VaR 10/50/90%=29.6/46.4/76.3; infinite ES=13/400

At k=400k=400k=400, 0 of 2,000 fits have infinite ES, a finite-sample count that does not establish zero population probability; the rule of three gives an approximate one-sided 95% upper confidence bound of 3/2000=0.15%3/2000=0.15\%3/2000=0.15%.

The same moment distinction applies to direct sample ES: observations can yield a finite numerical average even when the population ES is infinite. The point-forecast evaluation and plug-in mean pages explain the related loss-moment and boundary problems.
