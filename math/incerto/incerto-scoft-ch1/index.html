Chapter 1 — Prologue

Reading the Prologue¶

The Prologue asks what changes in statistical reasoning when extreme outcomes matter. Its terminology is deliberately broader than a single asymptotic tail class. We use that question to separate three properties: a large fourth moment relative to variance, the existence of exponential moments, and the scaling of a survival function.

The source is Taleb (2025), Statistical Consequences of Fat Tails, third edition, arXiv:2001.10488v4, chapter 1, printed pp. 3–6. This discussion chapter states no numbered mathematical theorem. The calculations below and in the tail class catalog are our own teaching examples; the Prologue is their motivation, not their proof source.

The source uses “thick” in a Gaussian-relative, kurtosis-based sense, and “fat” more broadly than a strict power-law definition (ch. 1, pp. 4–5). Here we name the mathematical property explicitly. The Cramér condition asks for a finite positive exponential moment; regular variation asks for a limiting multiplier ratio; subexponentiality asks about the tail of an iid sum. None is defined by the word “fat” alone.

High kurtosis with bounded support¶

For a variable with finite fourth moment and positive variance, define its Pearson kurtosis, locally denoted by KKK, as

K=E[(X−EX)4]Var⁡(X)2.K=\frac{\mathbb E[(X-\mathbb E X)^4]}{\operatorname{Var}(X)^2}.K=Var(X)2E[(X−EX)4]​.
(1)

This is kurtosis, not excess kurtosis K−3K-3K−3. A standard Gaussian has K=3K=3K=3: integration by parts with ϕ′(z)=−zϕ(z)\phi'(z)=-z\phi(z)ϕ′(z)=−zϕ(z) gives ∫z4ϕ(z) dz=3∫z2ϕ(z) dz=3\int z^4\phi(z)\,dz=3\int z^2\phi(z)\,dz=3∫z4ϕ(z)dz=3∫z2ϕ(z)dz=3, where both integrals run over R\mathbb RR and ϕ\phiϕ is the standard normal density.

We prove that arbitrarily large kurtosis is compatible with bounded support and finite exponential moments. Fix a local probability 0<ε≤10<\varepsilon\le10<ε≤1 and give XXX the three-point law

P(X=0)=1−ε,P(X=ε−1/2)=P(X=−ε−1/2)=ε2.P(X=0)=1-\varepsilon,\qquad P(X=\varepsilon^{-1/2})=P(X=-\varepsilon^{-1/2})=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.P(X=0)=1−ε,P(X=ε−1/2)=P(X=−ε−1/2)=2ε​.
(2)

Symmetry gives EX=0\mathbb E X=0EX=0. Summing over the three atoms gives

EX2=εε−1=1,EX4=εε−2=ε−1,K=ε−1.\mathbb E X^2=\varepsilon\varepsilon^{-1}=1,\qquad \mathbb E X^4=\varepsilon\varepsilon^{-2}=\varepsilon^{-1},\qquad K=\varepsilon^{-1}.EX2=εε−1=1,EX4=εε−2=ε−1,K=ε−1.
(3)

For every real θ\thetaθ, the same finite sum gives

EeθX=1−ε+εcosh⁡(θ/ε)<∞.\mathbb E e^{\theta X} =1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon\cosh(\theta/\sqrt{\varepsilon})<\infty.EeθX=1−ε+εcosh(θ/ε​)<∞.
(4)

For each fixed ε\varepsilonε the right tail ends at ε−1/2\varepsilon^{-1/2}ε−1/2. It therefore cannot have a regularly varying tail with positive exponent, or belong to the unbounded-support subexponential class used here. Choosing smaller ε\varepsilonε produces a different bounded law with larger kurtosis. It does not turn any one of these laws into a power-tailed distribution. For example, ε=0.01\varepsilon=0.01ε=0.01 gives variance 1, kurtosis 100, and support {−10,0,10}\{-10,0,10\}{−10,0,10}.

Ordinary moments and tail classes¶

The catalog supplies a second contrast: a lognormal variable has all positive power moments finite but has no finite positive exponential moment. It is subexponential, yet not regularly varying. An exact Pareto variable has a positive power moment of order qqq precisely when q<αq<\alphaq<α. The catalog proves the lognormal moment calculations and separates the cited classifications; the Pareto moment note owns the exact Pareto integral proof.

These examples answer different questions. A finite population variance requires a finite second moment. The kurtosis comparison requires a finite fourth moment as well. A finite Chernoff bound requires an exponential moment at the chosen positive parameter. A multiplier-ratio claim requires information arbitrarily far into the tail. A finite empirical fourth moment alone establishes none of those population assumptions.

Reproduce the model comparisons¶

From the installed repository environment, run:

Use bounded_spike_summary.

Code
from xmath.incerto.tail_classes import bounded_spike_summary

for epsilon in (0.1, 0.01, 0.001):
    print(f"epsilon={epsilon:.4g}")
    for name, value in bounded_spike_summary(epsilon).items():
        print(f"  {name}: {value:.4g}")

The script evaluates specified probability laws in dimensionless units. There are no observed data, random draws, fitted parameters, or confidence intervals. It prints the three-point calculation for ε∈{0.1,0.01,0.001}\varepsilon\in\{0.1,0.01,0.001\}ε∈{0.1,0.01,0.001} and the multiplier ratios for the catalog’s five unbounded models. A fixed 241-point grid spans 1≤u≤1041\le u\le10^41≤u≤104, with multiplier t=2t=2t=2; runtime is bounded by this grid and two static figure exports, normally a few seconds.

The catalog figure shows why a vanishing multiplier ratio cannot by itself distinguish lognormal and Weibull subexponential tails from exponential and gamma tails. The code uses log survival functions to avoid dividing two underflowed probabilities; it stops on non-finite values rather than dropping them. Finite curves illustrate the models and do not prove their limiting classes.
