Chapter 2 — Glossary, definitions, and notations

Reading a glossary through examples¶

A probability, a conditional mean, and a contribution to an expectation can all refer to observations above the same threshold. They answer different questions. This guide uses that distinction to connect the glossary to counting exceedances, the Pareto law, the extreme-value index, and the tail class catalog.

The source is Taleb (2025), Statistical Consequences of Fat Tails, third edition, arXiv:2001.10488v4, chapter 2, printed pp. 7–21. The tables give reading locations, not transcriptions of the glossary. Our examples and arguments are developed independently. The shared notation controls the symbols used here; source-specific conventions need translation.

A probability does not specify the tail payoff¶

For Pareto (α=3/2,xm=1)(\alpha=3/2,x_m=1)(α=3/2,xm​=1), threshold u=4u=4u=4 is the quantile Q(7/8)Q(7/8)Q(7/8). The exceedance probability is 1/81/81/8, but the conditional mean above that threshold is 12. The Pareto derivation gives mean excess 8 and tail contribution 3/23/23/2. All four numbers describe the same distribution and threshold.

For an integrable nonnegative loss, define expected shortfall at level 0<q<10<q<10<q<1 by (1−q)−1∫q1Q(v) dv(1-q)^{-1}\int_q^1Q(v)\,dv(1−q)−1∫q1​Q(v)dv. When there is no atom at the quantile, this agrees with the conditional tail mean. At an atom, conditioning on X>Q(q)X>Q(q)X>Q(q) generally selects a different probability mass. For the empirical law of (1,2,2,4)(1,2,2,4)(1,2,2,4) and q=1/2q=1/2q=1/2, the upper half consists of equal masses at 2 and 4, so the quantile integral gives 3. Strict conditioning on X>2X>2X>2 instead gives 4. This calculation explains why the probability level, sign convention, and treatment of ties must accompany the terms VaR and expected shortfall.

Even uniform CDF accuracy does not control a mean without additional tail conditions. To prove this, let PjP_jPj​ put mass 1−1/j1-1/j1−1/j at zero and 1/j1/j1/j at j2j^2j2, for integers j≥2j\ge2j≥2. Its CDF differs from that of a point mass at zero by at most 1/j1/j1/j, yet its mean is jjj. This deterministic sequence of laws is a counterexample to such an implication; it is not a simulation of an iid empirical process. It isolates the probability-versus-moment issue behind §§2.2.27–28, pp. 19–20, without importing a uniform convergence theorem.

Reproduce the worked cases¶

Use empirical_survival_function, pareto_tail_quantities.

Code
from xmath.incerto.stats import empirical_survival_function
from xmath.incerto.glossary import pareto_tail_quantities

support, survival = empirical_survival_function([1, 2, 2, 4])
print("Support: [" + ", ".join(f"{v:.4g}" for v in support) + "]")
print("Strict survival: [" + ", ".join(f"{v:.4g}" for v in survival) + "]")
for alpha in (0.8, 1, 1.5, 3):
    result = pareto_tail_quantities(alpha, x_m=1, threshold=4)
    print(f"alpha={alpha:.4g}")
    for name, value in result.items():
        print(f"  {name}: {value:.4g}")
