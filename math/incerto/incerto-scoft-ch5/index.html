Chapter 5 — Subexponentials and power laws

Chapter 5 compares two different tail properties. Subexponentiality describes a large sum of a fixed number of independent observations. Regular variation describes how survival changes when we multiply a threshold. The latter implies the former for nonnegative laws with positive tail exponent, but the converse fails.

We use Taleb (2025), third edition (2025), arXiv:2001.10488v4, chapter 5, printed pp. 91–105, and its attached appendix B, pp. 127–133. The notes develop their own examples and arguments. The source locators below identify context; each concept page identifies its proved and cited results.

Exponents need support and dependence assumptions¶

For nonnegative variables and positive fixed weights, a union bound and a one-coordinate lower bound preserve the smallest logarithmic tail exponent of a finite sum. That statement does not require independence. Signed variables can cancel: (V,−V)(V,-V)(V,−V) has sum zero even when both marginals have power tails. The sum proof states exactly what the bounds establish.

Products are more sensitive to dependence. Two independent unit-cutoff Pareto variables with common exponent α\alphaα have product survival

P(X1X2>z)=z−α(1+αlog⁡z),z≥1.P(X_1X_2>z)=z^{-\alpha}(1+\alpha\log z),\qquad z\ge1.P(X1​X2​>z)=z−α(1+αlogz),z≥1.

Qualifications to carry into the source¶

  • Exponential and gamma laws fail the convolution-subexponential criterion; the catalog’s direct proof takes precedence over treating them as members of that class.

  • A slowly varying factor need not approach a positive constant. The existing counterexample and the equal-exponent product above retain a logarithmic factor forever.

  • Matching a survival exponent, or even a leading survival constant, does not equate finite moments. Our double-Pareto density includes the required factor 1/21/21/2 on each side; its normalization and variance convention are explicit.

  • A limit E[X∣X>u]/u=1\mathbb E[X\mid X>u]/u=1E[X∣X>u]/u=1 does not establish a positive exponential moment. For survival e−ue^{-\sqrt u}e−u​, direct integration gives ∫u∞e−t dt=2(u+1)e−u\int_u^\infty e^{-\sqrt t}\,dt=2(\sqrt u+1)e^{-\sqrt u}∫u∞​e−t​dt=2(u​+1)e−u​. Dividing by survival gives e(u)=2u+2e(u)=2\sqrt u+2e(u)=2u​+2, so the conditional-mean ratio tends to one despite subexponentiality.

  • Maximum entropy is relative to a support, reference measure, and specified constraints. The appendix’s escort-mean calculation requires positive normalization and an admissible entropic index; stationarity alone is not a proof of a global optimum for a nonlinear constraint.

Notation follows the shared canon. The source’s lower cutoff LLL maps to xmx_mxm​; L(x)L(x)L(x) here is a slowly varying function. We reserve α>0\alpha>0α>0 for a survival exponent, use ν\nuν for Student degrees of freedom, and use local qqq for the entropic index. Reproduce the worked models¶

Run uv run python demos/incerto/power_laws.py after installing the repository dependencies. It produces four original figures: tail diagnostics, products and moment integrals, Student/double-Pareto matching, and entropy constraints. The grids and one-dimensional quadrature are bounded; no random draws, external data, fitted parameters, censoring, or sampling intervals are used. The concept-page captions give model parameters and numerical limits.

The maximum-entropy example keeps Elog⁡(X/xm)=1/2\mathbb E\log(X/x_m)=1/2Elog(X/xm​)=1/2 fixed and compares an exact Pareto law with a log-gamma alternative. The entropy gap is computed independently from the defining integrals. This checks a worked case of the analytic proof, not a general numerical search over densities.
