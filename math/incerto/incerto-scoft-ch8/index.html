Chapter 8 — How much averaging helps

An asymptotic limit does not tell us the error at a particular sample size. This chapter compares that error through kappa, the growth of mean absolute deviation under summation. The sample-sufficiency calculation then asks how many observations match a stated Gaussian reference.

This guide uses Taleb (2025), third edition (2025), arXiv:2001.10488v4, chapter 8, printed pp. 153–169. Section 8.6 is the chapter’s derivation appendix. It is distinct from the book’s lettered appendices. The attached earlier paper is Taleb (2018), arXiv:1802.05495v3; book locators below always refer to v4.

The source’s adjacent-size shorthand κ1\kappa_1κ1​ is κ1,2\kappa_{1,2}κ1,2​. Student degrees of freedom are ν\nuν; α\alphaα names the stable index or a stated power-tail exponent. The shared notation fixes sums and means. These distinctions prevent several incorrect inferences:

  • Stable sum growth n1/αn^{1/\alpha}n1/α gives κn0,n=2−α\kappa_{n_0,n}=2-\alphaκn0​,n​=2−α. The finite-first-moment endpoint excludes index one.

  • Kappa need not lie in [0,1][0,1][0,1], even for a uniquely unimodal density. The triangular example proves this without a simulation.

  • For cubic Student sums, our derivation gives log⁡(n)κ1,n→2log⁡(π/2)\log(n)\kappa_{1,n}\to2\log(\pi/2)log(n)κ1,n​→2log(π/2), correcting the printed constant in §8.6.1, p. 165. This is not a CLT error bound.

  • Matching absolute mean error does not match variance or confidence coverage. At Gaussian reference size 30, the exact cubic Student calculation gives size 44 under equal single-observation MADs. The value 120 in §8.3.2, p. 160 is not the minimum for this criterion.

  • Replacing a lognormal sum by a moment-matched lognormal is a heuristic. Our limits use direct expectation arguments; numerical estimates use the true sum model.

  • The portfolio calculation assumes independent, identically distributed, equally weighted exposures. It supplies no empirical stock kappas, optimal-allocation theorem, or general confidence-interval cutoff.

A worked comparison¶

Use gaussian_equivalent_n, student_sum_mad, and gamma_sum_mad.

from xmath.incerto.concentration import gaussian_equivalent_n, student_sum_mad, gamma_sum_mad

for name, mad in (("Student 3", student_sum_mad), ("Exponential", gamma_sum_mad)):
    n = gaussian_equivalent_n(mad, 30)
    print(f"{name}: n={n}, relative absolute error={mad(n)/(n*mad(1)):.4f}")

Output

Student 3: n=44, relative absolute error=0.1817
Exponential: n=36, relative absolute error=0.1803
