Subexponentiality

Statement¶

Let X1,X2,…X_1,X_2,\dotsX1​,X2​,… be iid nonnegative random variables with distribution FFF and unbounded right support, so Fˉ(x)=P(X>x)>0\bar F(x)=\mathbb P(X>x)>0Fˉ(x)=P(X>x)>0 for every finite xxx. The distribution is subexponential when, for two independent copies,

P(X1+X2>x)2P(X>x)→1,x→∞.\begin{gathered} \frac{\mathbb P(X_1+X_2>x)}{2\mathbb P(X>x)} \to 1,\\ x\to\infty. \end{gathered}2P(X>x)P(X1​+X2​>x)​→1,x→∞.​
(1)

Equivalently, for every fixed integer n≥2n\ge2n≥2 (and trivially for n=1n=1n=1), with Sn=X1+⋯+XnS_n=X_1+\cdots+X_nSn​=X1​+⋯+Xn​ and Mn=max⁡1≤i≤nXiM_n=\max_{1\le i\le n}X_iMn​=max1≤i≤n​Xi​,

P(Sn>x)∼P(Mn>x)∼nFˉ(x).\mathbb P(S_n>x) \sim \mathbb P(M_n>x) \sim n\bar F(x).P(Sn​>x)∼P(Mn​>x)∼nFˉ(x).
(2)

For those same fixed integers, equivalently,

P(Sn>x,  Mn≤x)=o(Fˉ(x)).\mathbb P(S_n>x,\; M_n\le x)=o(\bar F(x)).P(Sn​>x,Mn​≤x)=o(Fˉ(x)).
(3)

This is the one-big-jump principle: for a fixed number of summands, a very large sum is usually caused by one summand being very large, not by all summands moving moderately together.

The maximum-tail asymptotic P(Mn>x)∼nFˉ(x)\mathbb P(M_n>x)\sim n\bar F(x)P(Mn​>x)∼nFˉ(x) is not the distinctive part of the theorem: it follows for every iid distribution with an unbounded right tail and fixed nnn. The subexponential content is that the sum tail has the same first-order asymptotic as the maximum tail.

Regularly varying survival tails with positive exponent, Fˉ∈RV−α\bar F\in RV_{-\alpha}Fˉ∈RV−α​ for α>0\alpha>0α>0, are a standard subexponential class. We cite that theorem and use the exact Pareto family as a checkable example.

Here FFF is the common CDF, nnn is fixed while xxx grows, and α>0\alpha>0α>0 and xm>0x_m>0xm​>0 are the exponent and cutoff in the Pareto example. The notation a(x)∼b(x)a(x)\sim b(x)a(x)∼b(x) means a(x)/b(x)→1a(x)/b(x)\to1a(x)/b(x)→1; o(b(x))o(b(x))o(b(x)) denotes a term negligible after division by b(x)b(x)b(x).

One-big-jump intuition¶

Subexponential does not mean “lighter than exponential,” and it is unrelated to the concentration-theory phrase “sub-exponential random variable” for a light-tailed ψ1\psi_1ψ1​ condition. In this context it means the opposite: the tail is heavy enough that convolution barely changes the leading-order tail probability.

For two iid summands, a sufficiently large sum is about twice as likely as one summand exceeding the same threshold. For nnn fixed summands, the leading factor is nnn.

This is the mathematical version of a recurring Incerto warning. In thin-tail settings, a large aggregate deviation is often the result of many small coordinated deviations. In subexponential settings, the aggregate tail is usually dominated by a single large term. That changes how sums, ruin events, insurance losses, and sample moments should be read.

What we can prove directly¶

The statement collects several equivalent forms and one important sufficient class. We prove the elementary maximum-tail asymptotic and an exact Pareto two-summand check. The full equivalence theory and the theorem that regularly varying tails are subexponential are cited, because their proofs need more convolution-tail machinery than we develop here.

For the general fixed-nnn extension and its converse, we cite Foss, Korshunov, and Zachary (2013), §3.3, Corollary 3.20 and Theorem 3.21, p. 52. Their nonnegative case agrees with the definition here. The fixed-size Pareto proof for arbitrary nnn is already on the Pareto page.

The theorem that regularly varying tails are subexponential goes back to Chistyakov’s convolution-tail criterion for sums of independent positive random variables Chistyakov (1964). Standard modern references with proofs are Embrechts, Klueppelberg, and Mikosch, Appendix A3 Embrechts et al. (1997), and Foss, Korshunov, and Zachary, second edition, §3.5, Theorem 3.29 and its following paragraph, p. 57 Foss et al. (2013) for the regular-variation implication. We do not reprove the full theorem, but the exact Pareto case gives the right calibration.

Caveats¶

  • The definition above is for nonnegative iid summands. Two-sided or dependent data need extra assumptions before the one-big-jump reading is valid.

  • The equivalence for nnn summands holds for fixed nnn. It is not a uniform statement over arbitrary growing horizons.

  • Subexponentiality is a tail property. It does not say the body of the distribution is Pareto, nor does it choose an empirical threshold.

  • A simulation ratio near one is only a diagnostic. A durable mathematical claim needs a theorem, a checked distributional assumption, or a citation.
