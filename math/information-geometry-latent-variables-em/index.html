Latent variables and ordinary EM

A latent-variable model specifies a joint law for an observation XXX and an unobserved variable ZZZ. The likelihood of the observed data uses the marginal law of XXX. The EM algorithm relates these two likelihoods through the conditional distribution of ZZZ given the actual observations.

Joint, marginal, and posterior¶

Let Pθ(x,z)P_\theta(x,z)Pθ​(x,z) be a joint density with respect to fixed measures ν(dx)λ(dz)\nu(dx)\lambda(dz)ν(dx)λ(dz). Its marginal and posterior densities are

pθ(x)=∫Pθ(x,z) dλ(z),rθ(z∣x)=Pθ(x,z)pθ(x).p_\theta(x)=\int P_\theta(x,z)\,d\lambda(z),\qquad r_\theta(z\mid x)=\frac{P_\theta(x,z)}{p_\theta(x)}.pθ​(x)=∫Pθ​(x,z)dλ(z),rθ​(z∣x)=pθ​(x)Pθ​(x,z)​.
(1)

The posterior is defined where pθ(x)>0p_\theta(x)>0pθ​(x)>0. Integrals become sums for discrete latent variables. Assume throughout the following derivation that the densities have common positive support at the observations and that the log densities, posterior entropies, and KL terms used below are integrable.

For independent observations x1,…,xnx_1,\ldots,x_nx1​,…,xn​, the objective is the average observed log likelihood

ℓn(θ)=1n∑i=1nlog⁡pθ(xi).\ell_n(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\log p_\theta(x_i).ℓn​(θ)=n1​i=1∑n​logpθ​(xi​).
(2)

Even if log⁡Pθ(x,z)\log P_\theta(x,z)logPθ​(x,z) is easy to optimize, the integral inside log⁡pθ(x)\log p_\theta(x)logpθ​(x) may make direct optimization difficult.

A variational identity at each observation¶

For any conditional density qi(z)q_i(z)qi​(z) with the required support and finite terms, define

F(q,θ)=1n∑i=1n∫qi(z)log⁡Pθ(xi,z)qi(z) dλ(z).\mathcal F(q,\theta) =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \int q_i(z)\log\frac{P_\theta(x_i,z)}{q_i(z)}\,d\lambda(z).F(q,θ)=n1​i=1∑n​∫qi​(z)logqi​(z)Pθ​(xi​,z)​dλ(z).
(3)

KL divergence is DKL(q∥r)=∫qlog⁡(q/r) dλD_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|r)=\int q\log(q/r)\,d\lambdaDKL​(q∥r)=∫qlog(q/r)dλ, with natural logarithms. It is nonnegative: Jensen’s inequality applied to −log⁡-\log−log gives Eq[−log⁡(r/q)]≥−log⁡Eq[r/q]=0\mathbb E_q[-\log(r/q)]\ge-\log\mathbb E_q[r/q]=0Eq​[−log(r/q)]≥−logEq​[r/q]=0 when the supports agree. The more general support case gives the same inequality, or infinite KL if rrr vanishes on a set of positive qqq mass. Equality holds exactly when the two distributions agree.

Substituting Pθ(xi,z)=pθ(xi)rθ(z∣xi)P_\theta(x_i,z)=p_\theta(x_i)r_\theta(z\mid x_i)Pθ​(xi​,z)=pθ​(xi​)rθ​(z∣xi​) into F\mathcal FF gives the exact identity

ℓn(θ)=F(q,θ)+1n∑i=1nDKL ⁣(qi∥rθ(⋅∣xi)).\ell_n(\theta) =\mathcal F(q,\theta) +\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_i\|r_\theta(\cdot\mid x_i)\right).ℓn​(θ)=F(q,θ)+n1​i=1∑n​DKL​(qi​∥rθ​(⋅∣xi​)).
(4)

Thus F\mathcal FF is a lower bound on the likelihood, tight when each qiq_iqi​ is the posterior at the current parameter. This is the free-energy formulation of EM developed by Neal and Hinton in A view of the EM algorithm that justifies incremental, sparse, and other variants.

The two steps and the likelihood inequality¶

At iteration ttt, the E-step sets qt,i=rθt(⋅∣xi)q_{t,i}=r_{\theta_t}(\cdot\mid x_i)qt,i​=rθt​​(⋅∣xi​) and forms

Q(θ∣θt)=1n∑i=1nEθt ⁣[log⁡Pθ(xi,Z)∣X=xi].Q(\theta\mid\theta_t) =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb E_{\theta_t}\!\left[ \log P_\theta(x_i,Z)\mid X=x_i \right].Q(θ∣θt​)=n1​i=1∑n​Eθt​​[logPθ​(xi​,Z)∣X=xi​].
(5)

The expectation uses θt\theta_tθt​; the log joint density inside it uses the candidate parameter θ\thetaθ. During the M-step, keep the posterior fixed and choose a maximizer of Q(θ∣θt)Q(\theta\mid\theta_t)Q(θ∣θt​), assuming one exists in the specified parameter set. The entropy of qtq_tqt​ is constant in this optimization, so maximizing QQQ also maximizes F(qt,θ)\mathcal F(q_t,\theta)F(qt​,θ).

Equation (4) now gives

ℓn(θt+1)≥F(qt,θt+1)≥F(qt,θt)=ℓn(θt).\ell_n(\theta_{t+1}) \ge\mathcal F(q_t,\theta_{t+1}) \ge\mathcal F(q_t,\theta_t) =\ell_n(\theta_t).ℓn​(θt+1​)≥F(qt​,θt+1​)≥F(qt​,θt​)=ℓn​(θt​).
(6)

Increasing QQQ instead of maximizing it is enough for this inequality; that is a generalized EM step. Likelihood monotonicity does not establish a global maximum, convergence of the parameter sequence, or a convergence rate.

The E-step is also not, in general, a substitution Z←E[Z∣X]Z\leftarrow\mathbb E[Z\mid X]Z←E[Z∣X]. For a nonlinear term, such as Z2Z^2Z2 or log⁡Z\log ZlogZ, its posterior expectation must be computed separately. In particular, E[Z2∣X]=Var⁡(Z∣X)+E[Z∣X]2\mathbb E[Z^2\mid X]=\operatorname{Var}(Z\mid X)+\mathbb E[Z\mid X]^2E[Z2∣X]=Var(Z∣X)+E[Z∣X]2 when the second moment exists.

Exponential-family completion¶

Suppose the joint is a full regular minimal exponential family in natural coordinates:

Pθ(x,z)=h(x,z)exp⁡{θTT(x,z)−ψ(θ)}.P_\theta(x,z)=h(x,z) \exp\{\theta^\mathsf{T}T(x,z)-\psi(\theta)\}.Pθ​(x,z)=h(x,z)exp{θTT(x,z)−ψ(θ)}.
(11)

The E-step averages the entire sufficient-statistic vector:

m‾t=1n∑i=1nEθt[T(xi,Z)∣X=xi].\overline m_t=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb E_{\theta_t}[T(x_i,Z)\mid X=x_i].mt​=n1​i=1∑n​Eθt​​[T(xi​,Z)∣X=xi​].
(12)

Then Q(θ∣θt)=θTm‾t−ψ(θ)+constantQ(\theta\mid\theta_t)=\theta^\mathsf{T}\overline m_t-\psi(\theta) +\text{constant}Q(θ∣θt​)=θTmt​−ψ(θ)+constant. If an interior unconstrained M-step exists, the moment-matching equation becomes

η(θt+1)=∇ψ(θt+1)=m‾t.\eta(\theta_{t+1})=\nabla\psi(\theta_{t+1})=\overline m_t.η(θt+1​)=∇ψ(θt+1​)=mt​.
(13)

Constraints can change this equation, and the posterior moments need not lie in the attainable interior. These are optimization and model-domain questions, not consequences of the EM inequality.

The derivation used actual observations throughout. For continuous data, the empirical distribution is atomic and its KL to a continuous model is generally infinite. Ordinary EM requires no such KL. A later population projection construction must specify a suitable observable density and its support.
